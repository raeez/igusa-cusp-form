\chapter{K3 periods and the mirror discriminant}
\label{ch:view4-mirror}

\section{A geometric source for the Siegel period domain}
\label{sec:view4-mirror-discriminant}

The Siegel threefold already parametrizes a classical family of
lattice-polarized K3 surfaces. Its decomposable-abelian-surface divisor
is also a locus where the K3 polarization enlarges. This gives a
geometric interpretation of the zero divisor of \(\Delta_5\).
The further question is whether an independently defined compact
\(K3\times E\) construction produces the same period map, line and
meromorphic section. That comparison must retain the elliptic
variation and the geometric source of the section.

We first construct the K3 lattice and its period coordinates. The two
orbits of roots then distinguish the Humbert surfaces \(H_1\) and
\(H_4\). Finally we compare the local divisor on the period cover
with its coarse quotient. These calculations specify the target of
the compact mirror problem without assuming that problem solved.

\subsection{Polarization and the mirror lattice}
\label{subsec:k3-mirror-period}

Write
\[
 U=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
 \Lambda_{K3}=3U\oplus2E_8(-1),\qquad
 \widetilde\Lambda_{K3}=U_{\mathrm{Muk}}\oplus\Lambda_{K3}.
\]
The signatures are \((3,19)\) and \((4,20)\), respectively;
positive entries are counted first. The second lattice is the Mukai
lattice, with pairing
\[
 ((r,c,s),(r',c',s'))=c\cdot c'-rs'-r's
\]
\cite{Mukai1984,Mukai1987}.

Let \(M\) be an even lattice of signature \((1,t)\).
An \(M\)-polarization of a K3 surface \(S\) is a primitive embedding
\(M\hookrightarrow\operatorname{Pic}(S)\); we require its image to
contain a nef and big class, and call the polarization
\emph{pseudoample}. Requiring an ample class is stronger. A choice
of a primitive hyperbolic plane in \(M^\perp\subset\Lambda_{K3}\)
is additional data. Since \(U\) is unimodular, such a choice splits
\[
 M^\perp=U_{\mathrm{mir}}\oplus N.
\]
The lattice \(N\), of signature \((1,18-t)\), is the mirror lattice
for this choice \cite[\S\S1--3, 5]{DolgachevMirrorLP}.
Thus lattice polarization does not itself include a mirror cusp.

In the same K3 lattice, the complementary relation is
\[
 N^\perp=U_{\mathrm{mir}}\oplus M,
 \qquad \operatorname{sign}(N^\perp)=(2,t+1).
\]
Indeed, unimodularity of the chosen \(U_{\mathrm{mir}}\)
splits it from \(\Lambda_{K3}\). In the remaining summand,
\(M\) is primitive and \(N=M^\perp\); taking the orthogonal
complement again recovers \(M\).

For a fixed primitive embedding, the marked period domain is
\[
 \Omega_M=\{[\omega]\in\mathbb P(M^\perp\otimes\mathbb C):
       (\omega,\omega)=0,
       (\omega,\overline\omega)>0\}^{+}.
\]
The superscript selects one connected component. Its complex
 dimension is \(20-\operatorname{rk}M=19-t\).
The arithmetic group for polarizations fixed pointwise is the image
of the isometries of \(\Lambda_{K3}\) fixing \(M\). Equivalently it
is the component-preserving discriminant kernel in
\(O(M^\perp)\), with the relevant ample-chamber convention.
It need not be the full orthogonal group.

The lattice used here is
\[
 M=U\oplus\langle-2\rangle.
\]
Embed its rank-one summand as a root in one \(E_8(-1)\) factor of
\(\Lambda_{K3}\), and its hyperbolic plane in one of the three
\(U\)'s. The root complement is \(E_7(-1)\), so
\[
 M^\perp=2U\oplus E_8(-1)\oplus E_7(-1),\qquad
 N=U\oplus E_8(-1)\oplus E_7(-1).
\]
In particular, \(N\) has rank \(17\) and signature \((1,16)\).
Its orthogonal complement in the same K3 lattice is
\[
 T=N^\perp=2U\oplus\langle-2\rangle,
 \qquad \operatorname{sign}(T)=(2,3).
\]
The domain \(\Omega_M\) has dimension \(17\), whereas
\(\Omega_N\subset\mathbb P(T\otimes\mathbb C)\) has dimension
\(3\). They parametrize different lattice-polarized K3 surfaces.

\subsection{The integral gluing}
\label{subsec:view4-lattice-check}

The root complement above is integral, but the root and its
orthogonal complement do not split \(E_8\) integrally. An explicit
basis exhibits the difference. In Euclidean \(\mathbb R^8\), set
\[
 \begin{split}
 a_1&=\tfrac12(e_1-e_2-e_3-e_4-e_5-e_6-e_7+e_8),\\
 a_2&=e_1+e_2,\qquad a_3=-e_1+e_2,\\
 a_j&=-e_{j-2}+e_{j-1}\qquad(4\le j\le8).
 \end{split}
\]
Their Gram matrix is the positive \(E_8\) matrix, of determinant
\(1\). The first seven roots have the \(E_7\) Gram matrix, of
 determinant \(2\). They are orthogonal to \(r=e_7+e_8\), and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:view4-e8-glue}
 r=2a_1+3a_2+4a_3+6a_4+5a_5+4a_6+3a_7+2a_8.
\end{equation}
Consequently
\[
 [E_8:E_7\oplus\mathbb Zr]=2,
 \qquad \det(E_7\oplus\mathbb Zr)=4.
\]
Indeed, replacing the last basis vector \(a_8\) by \(r\) has
 determinant \(2\). Equation~\eqref{eq:view4-e8-glue} also displays
 the missing glue vector. Reversing the form gives the asserted
 embedding into \(E_8(-1)\).

This distinction matters when a root becomes algebraic. A primitive
closure can enlarge \(E_7(-1)\oplus\langle-2\rangle\) to
\(E_8(-1)\). Adding the two orthogonal lattices without their glue
would give the wrong discriminant group.

\section{The period map and an explicit K3 family}
\label{subsec:view4-period-coordinates}

Choose a basis \((e_1,f_1,e_2,f_2,g)\) of \(T\) with
\((e_i,f_i)=1\), \(g^2=-2\), and all other pairings zero.
For
\[
 Z=\begin{pmatrix}\tau&z\\z&\sigma\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb H_2,
 \qquad
 w(Z)=e_1+(z^2-\tau\sigma)f_1+\tau e_2+\sigma f_2+zg,
\]
one has
\begin{equation}\label{eq:view4-period-vector}
 (w(Z),w(Z))=0,\qquad
 (w(Z),\overline{w(Z)})=4\det\operatorname{Im}Z>0.
\end{equation}
The first identity follows by canceling
\(2(z^2-\tau\sigma)+2\tau\sigma-2z^2\).
For the second, write \(\operatorname{Im}\tau=b\),
\(\operatorname{Im}\sigma=d\), \(\operatorname{Im}z=f\);
the pairing is \(4(bd-f^2)\).
The tube-domain parametrization \(Z\mapsto[w(Z)]\) identifies
\(\mathbb H_2\) with the selected component \(\Omega_N\).

The discriminant group of \(T\) is \(\mathbb Z/2\), whose
quadratic-form automorphism group is trivial. Thus the
component-preserving discriminant kernel here is \(O^+(T)\).
Its effective projective action is the genus-two Siegel modular
action, with real exceptional isomorphism
\(\operatorname{PSp}_4(\mathbb R)\simeq
\operatorname{SO}_0(T\otimes\mathbb R)\).
The K3 Torelli theorem gives the coarse period isomorphism
\begin{equation}\label{eq:view4-period-bridge}
 \mathcal M_N\simeq
 O^+(T)\backslash\Omega_N
 \simeq\operatorname{Sp}_4(\mathbb Z)\backslash\mathbb H_2,
\end{equation}
where \(\mathcal M_N\) denotes the coarse moduli space of
pseudoample \(N\)-polarized K3 surfaces
\cite[Proposition 3.1]{ClingherDoran2012}.
The quotient on the right also parametrizes principally polarized
abelian surfaces. The associated Hodge correspondence is the
classical K3--abelian-surface correspondence of
\cite[Proposition 3.2]{ClingherDoran2012}.

Here is a family realizing the K3 side. For complex parameters
\((\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta)\) with
\((\gamma,\delta)\ne(0,0)\), consider the quartic in
\(\mathbb P^3_{[x:y:z:w]}\)
\begin{equation}\label{eq:view4-quartic}
 \begin{split}
 y^2zw-4x^3z+3\alpha xzw^2+\beta zw^3
   +\gamma xz^2w
   -\tfrac12(\delta z^2w^2+w^4)=0.
 \end{split}
\end{equation}
In this equation \(z\) is a projective coordinate, distinct from the
 off-diagonal period coordinate used above.
The minimal resolution is a K3 surface with a canonical
pseudoample \(N\)-polarization. Every such polarized surface occurs,
and its isomorphism class is determined by
\[
 [\alpha:\beta:\gamma:\delta]\in\mathbb P(2,3,5,6),
 \qquad (\gamma,\delta)\ne(0,0)
\]
\cite[Theorems 1.2 and 1.4]{ClingherDoran2012}.
The construction identifies the two exceptional root configurations
of types \(E_8\) and \(E_7\) together with the hyperbolic plane of
an elliptic fibration. The minimal resolution, rather than the
singular quartic itself, is the smooth K3 surface.

The weights can be checked directly. Replacing the parameters by
\((t^4\alpha,t^6\beta,t^{10}\gamma,t^{12}\delta)\) and the
coordinates by \((t^2x,t^3y,t^{-6}z,w)\) leaves every term in
\eqref{eq:view4-quartic} unchanged. Over \(\mathbb C\), this
realizes the weighted scaling \((2,3,5,6)\).

In the modular normalizations of Clingher--Doran, the inverse period
map is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:view4-inverse-period}
 Z\longmapsto
 [\mathcal E_4(Z):\mathcal E_6(Z):
       2^{12}3^5\mathcal C_{10}(Z):
       2^{12}3^6\mathcal C_{12}(Z)]
\end{equation}
\cite[Theorem 3.5]{ClingherDoran2012}.
Their proof compares two elliptic fibrations on the associated
Kummer surface: one obtained by a Nikulin involution of
\eqref{eq:view4-quartic}, the other from genus-two theta functions.
Matching the Weierstrass coefficients gives
\eqref{eq:view4-inverse-period}.
The normalization of the weight-ten coordinate is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:view4-cd-normalization}
 \mathcal C_{10}=-2^{-14}\Delta_5^2=-\tfrac14\Xi,
 \qquad \Xi=64^{-2}\Delta_5^2.
\end{equation}
This follows from their squared theta-product formula
\cite[(34)]{ClingherDoran2012}; the phase change in
Section~\ref{sec:view1-theta-normalization} has square one.
Thus their \(\mathcal C_{10}\) must not be substituted for the
monic counting form \(\Xi\) without its scalar.

A family of smooth resolutions is taken on a suitable marked or
level cover. Its product with a fixed elliptic curve is a compact
family of threefolds with trivial canonical bundle. This is already
a positive geometric construction. Comparing it with a prescribed
compact Hall theory, or allowing a specified varying elliptic
factor, requires further maps.

\subsection{Forms, sign conventions and Hodge lines}
\label{subsec:view4-volIII-lattice-convention}

The orthogonal lattice of the preceding calculation has signature
\((2,3)\). The exterior-square model of
Section~\ref{ssec:exceptional-iso} uses signature \((3,2)\).
To pass between them, negate the entire bilinear form and then
identify \(-U\simeq U\) in each hyperbolic factor. This sends
\(T\) to a lattice isometric to \(2U\oplus\langle2\rangle\).
It is an identification with \(T(-1)\), not an isometry from
\(T\) to a lattice of different signature. The domain inequality
changes from positive to negative, and a root of square \(-2\)
becomes a vector of square \(2\). The orthogonal hyperplane is
unchanged.

On the marked domain, the K3 Hodge line is
\(\lambda_N=\mathbb Cw(Z)\). The primitive exterior-square
construction identifies this line with the determinant of the
abelian surface's holomorphic one-forms. An equivariant line
comparison requires the corresponding arithmetic actions. In
particular, the coarse isomorphism
\eqref{eq:view4-period-bridge} does not by itself identify the two
moduli stacks or all of their stabilizer actions on Hodge lines.
We use a common level cover whenever an actual line is required.

Let \(\lambda_{\mathrm{aut}}\) be the weight-one automorphic line
on that cover, and let \(\varepsilon_5\) be the character line of
\(\Delta_5\). Then
\[
 \Delta_5\in H^0(\lambda_{\mathrm{aut}}^{\otimes5}
                         \otimes\varepsilon_5),\qquad
 \Delta_5^2\in H^0(\lambda_{\mathrm{aut}}^{\otimes10}),
 \qquad \varepsilon_5^{\otimes2}\simeq\mathcal O.
\]
The character belongs to the full automorphy law. Its slash-action
value need not equal the sign of a function in a chosen linear tube
frame; Section~\ref{sec:view1-theta-normalization} and the character
calculation of Chapter~\ref{ch:view1-automorphic} fix that convention.

\section{The two root divisors}
\label{subsec:view4-discriminant}

For a primitive root \(v\in T\), put
\[
 H_v=\{[\omega]\in\Omega_N:(\omega,v)=0\},\qquad
 \operatorname{div}(v)=\gcd\{(v,x):x\in T\}.
\]
These are rational quadratic, or Heegner, divisors
\cite[\S2]{BruinierFunkeHeegner}. The roots of \(T\) form two
orthogonal-group orbits, distinguished by divisibility
\cite[\S3.3]{ClingherDoran2012}.
Their simplest representatives are
\[
 v_2=g,\qquad v_1=e_2-f_2.
\]
Both have square \(-2\), while
\[
 \operatorname{div}(v_2)=2,\quad (w(Z),v_2)=-2z;
 \qquad
 \operatorname{div}(v_1)=1,\quad (w(Z),v_1)=\sigma-\tau.
\]
Their orthogonal complements in \(T\) are
\(2U\) and \(U\oplus\langle2\rangle\oplus\langle-2\rangle\),
respectively. On the Siegel quotient their images are
\[
 H_1:\ z=0,\qquad H_4:\ \tau=\sigma,
\]
with the full arithmetic orbits understood. The subscript is the
Humbert discriminant. The decomposable locus is \(H_1\), not
\(H_2\).

To retain the root-type notation, write
\begin{equation}\label{eq:view4-discriminant-divisor}
 \mathfrak H_{\mathrm{II}}
   =\Bigl(\bigcup_{v^2=-2,\ \operatorname{div}(v)=2}H_v\Bigr)
       /O^+(T)=H_1.
\end{equation}
A point of \(H_1\) corresponds to a product of two elliptic curves.
On the K3 side, its \(N\)-polarization extends to
\(U\oplus E_8(-1)\oplus E_8(-1)\).
The index-two glue above explains the second \(E_8\).
The other root orbit gives the \(N\oplus\langle-2\rangle\)
enhancement along \(H_4\). Thus the full root arrangement has two
types, whereas \(\Delta_5\) detects the divisibility-two type.
Neither type should be identified with singular fibres of every
chosen K3 family: an extra algebraic root and a singular uncontracted
K3 surface are different conditions.

\subsection{Orders on the period cover and the coarse quotient}
\label{subsec:view4-divisor-orders}

Let \(\widetilde H_1\) be the reduced union of the divisibility-two
hyperplanes in \(\mathbb H_2\). The Borcherds product and theta
normalization give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:view4-divisor-of-Delta5}
 \operatorname{div}_{\mathbb H_2}(\Delta_5)=\widetilde H_1,
 \qquad
 \operatorname{div}_{\mathbb H_2}(\Delta_5^{-2})=-2\widetilde H_1.
\end{equation}
These are divisors in the open domain, before adding a toroidal
boundary \cite{GN,GNII,B}.
If \(\operatorname{Pole}(s)\) denotes the effective polar divisor
of a meromorphic section \(s\), then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:view4-conjecture}
 \operatorname{Pole}_{\mathbb H_2}(\Delta_5^{-2})=2\widetilde H_1.
\end{equation}
The sign differs from the signed divisor of the section.

Near a generic point of \(z=0\), take the normal coordinate
\(u=z\). The root reflection sends \(u\) to \(-u\), and the
local theta product has the form
\[
 \Delta_5=u\,a(u),\qquad a(0)\ne0.
\]
Its odd transformation in this frame is part of the line action.
On the effective coarse reflection quotient the normal coordinate is
\(t=u^2\), so
\[
 \Delta_5^2=t\,b(t),\qquad b(0)\ne0.
\]
Consequently the square has zero order one, and its inverse has pole
order one, along the generic coarse Humbert divisor. On the period
cover, or as a stack divisor pulled back to it, the corresponding
orders are two. A root line with nontrivial reflection action does
not descend as an ordinary line on the same coarse chart.

The distinction is visible without a global product formula:
\(\operatorname{ord}_{u}(t)=2\). The reflection records the
ramification. It does not determine a distinguished meromorphic
section or its order. In particular, Picard--Lefschetz monodromy
alone cannot prove an order-two pole for a geometric discriminant.

The two integers appearing here have different sources. The
Borcherds weight \(f(0,0)/2=5\) comes from the constant Jacobi
coefficient. The Weyl vector specifies the cusp exponents. Neither
the weight nor the reflection alone replaces the full boundary
factor of Chapter~\ref{ch:view1-automorphic}.

\section{What remains for a compact mirror comparison}
\label{subsec:k3xe-period-family}

For \(X=S\times E\), K\"unneth gives
\[
 H^3(X,\mathbb Z)=H^2(S,\mathbb Z)\otimes H^1(E,\mathbb Z),
 \qquad \operatorname{rk}H^3(X)=44.
\]
This is a weight-three Hodge structure with alternating
polarization. In contrast,
\[
 H^{\mathrm{even}}(X,\mathbb Z)
   =\widetilde H(S,\mathbb Z)\otimes
       \bigl(H^0(E,\mathbb Z)\oplus H^2(E,\mathbb Z)\bigr)
\]
is an integral group of rank \(48\).
The two even elliptic summands have constant Hodge type. They do not
contain the varying elliptic modulus, which belongs to \(H^1(E)\).
Identifying their underlying rank-two lattice with a hyperbolic
plane supplies no elliptic period map.

\begin{proposition}[A restriction on a Hodge-quotient construction]
\label{prop:view4-weight-three-obstruction}
A polarized weight-three variation cannot have a rank-five
polarized quotient with nondegenerate induced polarization.
No integral Tate twist of a weight-three variation has weight two.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The polarization in odd weight is alternating. If \(A\) is an
alternating matrix of odd size over a field of characteristic zero,
then
\(\det A=\det A^t=\det(-A)=-\det A\), so \(\det A=0\).
A rank-five alternating polarization is therefore degenerate.
An integral Tate twist changes the weight by an even integer,
which proves the second assertion.
\end{proof}

This excludes the specified polarized-quotient route from
\(H^3(X)\) to \(T\). It does not exclude a different geometric
construction of a period map. The classical \(N\)-polarized K3
family is already such a period construction, with a different
input. Nor does the dimension difference between \(\Omega_M\)
and \(\Omega_N\) prohibit every correspondence. It shows why
mirror lattices alone cannot specify one.

We use the notation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:view4-k3e-moduli}
 \mathcal P_{K3\times E}^{(3)}\longrightarrow
 \mathcal M_N\simeq\mathcal A_2
\end{equation}
only for a proposed three-dimensional compact period construction
with this actual comparison map supplied.
The superscript denotes a dimension, not the rank of a
weight-two period lattice; that lattice has rank five.
It is not a definition of a quotient of \(H^3(X)\) or of the
rank-48 even cohomology group.

\subsection{The line and section problem}
\label{subsec:view4-conjecture}

A compact mirror comparison would start with a geometric family,
a period morphism \(p:B\to\mathcal A_2\), and a geometric Hodge
or determinant line \(\lambda_{\mathrm{per}}\) on an explicitly
chosen base or stack. It would then supply a meromorphic section
\(s_{\mathrm{mir}}\) and a line isomorphism relating it to the
inverse Igusa section. This order prevents the desired modular
form from serving as the definition of its own geometric source.

\begin{conjecture}[Compact mirror discriminant comparison]
\label{conj:mirror-discriminant}
For a suitable mirror construction associated with the compact
\(K3\times E\) theory, there exist the preceding family, period
map and geometric section, together with
\[
 \iota:p^*\lambda_{\mathrm{aut}}^{-10}
             \xrightarrow{\ \sim\ }\lambda_{\mathrm{per}}^{-10},
 \qquad
 \iota(p^*\Delta_5^{-2})=s_{\mathrm{mir}}.
\]
The comparison is required to extend across the chosen
discriminant and boundary, with the same cusp normalization.
The elliptic parameter and the comparison with the original compact
geometric source are part of the construction.
\end{conjecture}

On a common period cover on which the reduced discriminant
\(\widetilde{\mathfrak D}_{\mathrm{mir}}\) pulls back to
\(\widetilde H_1\), and where the period map is unramified normally,
the predicted polar-divisor equality is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:view4-divisor-equation}
 \operatorname{Pole}(s_{\mathrm{mir}})
       =2\widetilde{\mathfrak D}_{\mathrm{mir}}.
\end{equation}
For a ramified period map the orders acquire its ramification
indices. On the generic effective coarse reflection quotient the
corresponding order is one. These statements fix the base before
assigning a multiplicity.

Matching divisors is weaker than matching sections. After the
comparison of lines, their quotient can be a nonconstant invertible
function on an open base. A boundary extension and a normalization
must eliminate that ambiguity. Likewise a square-line comparison
does not construct a square root with the required character.

\subsection{The four independent period conditions}
\label{subsec:view4-period-components}

The geometric comparison separates into the following conditions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(M1)] Construct the period morphism and the comparison of
  lines, with their coefficient, marking and stabilizer conventions.
  On the open complement of the discriminant this trivializes
  \(p^*\lambda_{\mathrm{aut}}^{-10}
     \otimes\lambda_{\mathrm{per}}^{10}\).
\item[(M2)] Extend the section comparison over the discriminant
  and a specified boundary. Compute its local orders on that base
  and its full cusp expansion; the weight five alone is insufficient.
\item[(M3)] Relate the chosen mirror construction to the original
  compact source, including the varying elliptic factor. Dolgachev's
  lattice construction and the complexified K\"ahler interpretation
  of \cite{AspinwallMorrisonStringy} do not furnish a canonical map
  between moduli spaces of dimensions seventeen and three, or an
  involution of either space.
\item[(M4)] For another polarization, specify the new orthogonal
  lattice, arithmetic group, automorphic input and geometric line.
  Its higher-rank extension is a new comparison problem. The
  classical rank-three denominator examples of \cite{GNII}, the
  reflective-product constructions of \cite{GC}, and the proposed
  lattice-polarized families of \cite{CYQGVolIIILatticePolarized}
  do not impose a universal Picard-rank criterion for this comparison.
\end{enumerate}

The broader Borcherds construction supplies many possible
orthogonal automorphic targets \cite{Borcherds95,BorcherdsGKM88}.
For example, the fake-monster root lattice has signature
\((25,1)\), whereas its weight-twelve orthogonal form lives on a
lattice of signature \((26,2)\), or \((2,26)\) after reversing the
form. It is a different target from \(T\). Any restriction or
quasi-pullback must specify its embedding and the extra root factors;
it is not the period comparison of
Conjecture~\ref{conj:mirror-discriminant} by itself.

\subsection{Curve counts, Pfaffians and states}
\label{subsec:view4-not-asserted}

The primitive reduced curve-counting theorem gives, in the
conventions fixed in Chapter~\ref{ch:zbps-realization},
\[
 Z^X_{\mathrm{OP}}=-\Xi^{-1}=-4096\Delta_5^{-2}
\]
\cite{BRYAN,OB}. The underlying generating series and its rational
expansion are the enumerative assertion. The rescaled scalar
\(-4096^{-1}Z^X_{\mathrm{OP}}=\Delta_5^{-2}\) does not on its own
specify an operator trace or a geometric discriminant section.
Conjecture~\ref{conj:mirror-discriminant} would give a period-side
interpretation of that scalar if its geometric construction and
normalization were supplied.

The automorphic product, the denominator superalgebra and the
regularized theta lift are established at their stated recipients.
The compact Pfaffian identity additionally needs a geometric skew
family, an orientation and a comparison of sections. Its four
conditions \((\mathrm{D0}), (\mathrm{O1}), (\mathrm{O1}^{+}),
(\mathrm{O2})\) govern those objects, whereas \((\mathrm M1)\)--
\((\mathrm M4)\) govern the period family and its section.
Neither collection of conditions supplies the other.
A gravitational or boundary interpretation further requires its
state, supported operations, trace domain and sewing maps
\cite{ChiralBarCobarVolII,MixedHTSCompanion,CYQGVolIII}.

The numerical tuple \((0,0,3,5,24)\) retains these distinctions.
The first two entries are the holomorphic Euler characteristics of
the compact product, the third is the Cartan rank of the reference
root algebra, the fourth is its Borcherds weight, and the last is
the Mukai rank of a K3 surface. A proposed Hall specialization must
compare its own Cartan object before using the third entry as a
geometric rank. The tuple is not a relation among these invariants
and does not construct a mirror period map.

The classical K3 period model and its root divisors therefore remain
available as positive geometric input. The compact problem asks for
the additional map from independently defined geometry, with its
line, section and elliptic variation, to that explicit target.
