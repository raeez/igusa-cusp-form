\chapter{The denominator algebra}
\label{ch:view2-bkm}

The product expansion of the Igusa form assigns an integer to every
positive lattice degree. To obtain a Lie superalgebra, one must also
specify generators and relations. At the first interior degree, the product exponent is $-64$.
The algebra constructed below has $29$ even and $93$ odd vectors there.
Their difference explains the exponent. Their brackets require the
presentation.

Gritsenko and Nikulin construct this presentation by adjoining
imaginary simple generators to a rank-three hyperbolic Kac--Moody
algebra \cite[Sections~2--3 and~6]{GN}. We first describe its lattice
and chamber, then extract the imaginary generators from the chamber
Fourier expansion. The resulting superdenominator is
$64^{-1}\Delta_5(2Z)$. Its ordinary denominator supplies the additional
information needed to recover both parity dimensions at every degree.

Finite root spaces then provide concrete comparison spaces for the
compact $K3\times E$ problem. The comparison itself must preserve
brackets, pairings and coefficient objects. Equality of denominators
supplies only its numerical consequence.

\section{The lattice and its chamber}
\label{sec:bkm-lattice}

Write $U$ for the integral hyperbolic plane, with isotropic generators
whose pairing is $-1$. The lattice relevant to the chosen cusp is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bkm-lambda21-def}
 \La^{2,1}=U\oplus\langle2\rangle
 =\Z f_2\oplus\Z f_3\oplus\Z f_{-2},
\end{equation}
with bilinear form
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bkm-lambda21-form}
 (f_2,f_{-2})=-1,\qquad (f_3,f_3)=2,\qquad
 (f_2,f_2)=(f_{-2},f_{-2})=(f_2,f_3)=(f_{-2},f_3)=0.
\end{equation}
It is even of signature $(2,1)$. Adjoining a second hyperbolic plane
gives the orthogonal lattice $\La^{3,2}$ used in
Section~\ref{ssec:exceptional-iso}.

\begin{definition}[Type-II sublattice]
\label{def:bkm-type-ii-sublattice}
The type-II lattice is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bkm-type-ii-def}
 L:=\La^{2,1}_{II}
 =2\Z f_2\oplus\Z f_3\oplus2\Z f_{-2}.
\end{equation}
Its dual and discriminant group are
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bkm-type-ii-dual}
 L^*=\tfrac12\La^{2,1},\qquad
 L^*/L\simeq\Z/4\oplus\Z/2\oplus\Z/4.
\end{equation}
Thus $[\La^{2,1}:L]=4$, $[L^*:\La^{2,1}]=8$ and
$L\simeq U(4)\oplus\langle2\rangle$, with determinant $-32$.
\end{definition}

The three vectors
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bkm-type-ii-simple-roots}
 \delta_1=2f_2-f_3,\qquad
 \delta_2=2f_{-2}-f_3,\qquad \delta_3=f_3
\end{equation}
form an integral basis of $L$. They have square two and divisibility
two in $\La^{2,1}$: the ideal $(\delta_i,\La^{2,1})$ is $2\Z$.

\begin{lemma}[Cartan matrix]
\label{lem:bkm-cartan-matrix}
The Gram matrix of this basis is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bkm-cartan-matrix}
 A=((\delta_i,\delta_j))
 =\begin{pmatrix}2&-2&-2\\-2&2&-2\\-2&-2&2\end{pmatrix}
 =4I_3-2J_3,
\end{equation}
where $J_3$ is the all-ones matrix. Its eigenvalues are $-2,4,4$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For example,
$(2f_2-f_3,2f_{-2}-f_3)=-4+2=-2$.
The other entries follow from~\eqref{eq:bkm-lambda21-form}.
The line spanned by $(1,1,1)$ has eigenvalue $-2$. The plane of
coordinate sum zero has eigenvalue $4$.
\end{proof}

The Gram-index lattice of Chapter~\ref{ch:k3xe-setup} is
$\Gamma_{\mathrm{ind}}=\Z^3$. Its identification with $L$ is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bkm-alpha-map}
 \alpha(n,l,m)=2nf_2-lf_3+2mf_{-2}
 =n\delta_1+m\delta_2+(n+m-l)\delta_3.
\end{equation}
If $\gamma=(n,l,m)$ and $\gamma'=(n',l',m')$, then
\begin{align}
\label{eq:bkm-gram-index-pairing}
 \langle\gamma,\gamma'\rangle_{\mathrm{ind}}
 &=2(nm'+n'm)-ll',\\
\label{eq:bkm-pullback-pairing}
 (\alpha(\gamma),\alpha(\gamma'))
 &=-2\langle\gamma,\gamma'\rangle_{\mathrm{ind}},\qquad
 \alpha(\gamma)^2=-2(4nm-l^2).
\end{align}
This integral map is fixed throughout the chapter. In particular,
changing an exponential coordinate does not change its image lattice.

\subsection{Reflections and the positive cone}
\label{sec:bkm-walls-chamber}

For a positive-norm vector $\beta$, its orthogonal reflection is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bkm-kac-reflection}
 s_\beta(x)=x-\frac{2(x,\beta)}{(\beta,\beta)}\beta.
\end{equation}
It preserves an integral lattice when
$(\beta,\beta)$ divides $2(\beta,L)$. For a square-two vector the
formula is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bkm-square-2-reflection}
 s_\beta(x)=x-(x,\beta)\beta.
\end{equation}
Let $V_+$ be the component of $\{x\in L_\R:x^2<0\}$ containing
$\delta_1+\delta_2+\delta_3$. We also write
$\Cone(\La^{2,1})_+=V_+$.

\begin{definition}[Type-II Weyl chamber]
\label{def:bkm-type-ii-chamber}
The chamber and its closed cone are
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bkm-poly-ii}
 \Poly_{II}=\{\R_{>0}x:x\in V_+,\ (x,\delta_i)\leq0\ (1\leq i\leq3)\},
 \qquad
 \mathcal C=\{x\in L_\R:(x,\delta_i)\leq0\ (1\leq i\leq3)\}.
\end{equation}
The primitive wall normals are
$\Poly_{II,\mathrm{prim}}=\{\delta_1,\delta_2,\delta_3\}$.
The notation $\R_{>0}\Poly_{II}$ will include the nonzero ideal
boundary of $\mathcal C$.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[Chamber and dual cone]
\label{lem:bkm-chamber-dual-cone}
Put $Q_+=\N\delta_1+\N\delta_2+\N\delta_3$ and
$\Delta(L)_+=\R_{\geq0}Q_+$. With the nonpositive pairing convention
for the dual cone, one has
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bkm-chamber-dual-cone}
 \Delta(L)_+^*=\mathcal C
 \subset\overline V_+\subset\Delta(L)_+.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Write $x=\sum_i b_i\delta_i$ and $t_i=-(x,\delta_i)$.
Since $A^{-1}=(I_3-J_3)/4$, the chamber inequalities give
\[
 b_i=\frac{\sum_{j\ne i}t_j}{4}\geq0,\qquad
 x^2=-\frac12\sum_{i<j}t_it_j\leq0.
\]
A nonzero such vector belongs to the future component. This proves
the first inclusion.

For the second, let $s=\sum_i b_i\geq0$ on
$\overline V_+$. The norm inequality reads
$2\sum_i b_i^2\leq s^2$. If $b_1<0$, then
\[
 \sum_i b_i^2\geq b_1^2+\tfrac12(s-b_1)^2>\tfrac12s^2,
\]
a contradiction. The other coordinates are nonnegative by symmetry.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Divisibility classes]
\label{rem:bkm-divisibility-types}
A primitive square-two vector in $\La^{2,1}$ has divisibility one
or two, called type~I or type~II. To see the parity distinction,
write it as $af_2+bf_3+cf_{-2}$. Its norm equation is $b^2-ac=1$.
The divisibility is $\gcd(a,2b,c)$. It is two precisely when $a,c$
are even, in which case $b$ is odd.

Otherwise $a+b+c$ is even.
Thus type~I vectors lie in
\[
 \La^{2,1}_I=\{af_2+bf_3+cf_{-2}:a+b+c\equiv0\pmod2\},
\]
and type~II vectors lie in $L$. The vectors
$f_{-2}-f_2,f_2-f_3,f_{-2}-f_3$ are type~I. The $\delta_i$ are
type~II. The divisor of $\Delta_5$ uses the latter orbit, as proved
in Chapter~\ref{ch:view1-automorphic}.
\end{remark}

\begin{lemma}[Reflection groups]
\label{lem:bkm-reflection-decomposition}
Let $W^{(2)}(\La^{2,1})$ be generated by its square-two reflections,
and let $W_I,W_{II}$ be generated by the type~I and type~II
reflections. Then
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bkm-reflection-indices}
 \Orth(\La^{2,1})_+=W^{(2)}(\La^{2,1}),\qquad
 [W^{(2)}:W_I]=2,\qquad [W^{(2)}:W_{II}]=6,
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bkm-chamber-decomposition}
 W^{(2)}(\La^{2,1})=W_{II}\rtimes\Aut(\Poly_{II}),\qquad
 \Aut(\Poly_{II})\simeq S_3.
\end{equation}
The group $W_{II}$ is the Coxeter group on $s_{\delta_1},s_{\delta_2},
s_{\delta_3}$ with relations $s_{\delta_i}^2=1$ only.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
These are the reflection decompositions for $U\oplus\langle2\rangle$
in \cite[Section~2, equations~(2.1)--(2.3)]{GN}, using Nikulin's
classification \cite{NIKULIN}. The type-II chamber is an ideal
triangle: each pair of its wall normals has Gram matrix
$\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&-2\\-2&2\end{smallmatrix}\right)$.
Consequently distinct simple reflections have no finite braid
relation. The chamber stabilizer permutes its three ideal vertices,
and the three reflections in the following lemma realize all these
permutations. Its order gives the index six. The type-I reflection
subgroup has the separate order-two chamber symmetry described in
the cited decomposition.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[$S_3$ chamber action]
\label{lem:bkm-chamber-s3}
The reflections in $f_{-2}-f_2$, $f_2-f_3$ and $f_{-2}-f_3$
act on $(\delta_1,\delta_2,\delta_3)$ as $(12)$, $(13)$ and $(23)$,
respectively.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For the first reflection, $f_2$ and $f_{-2}$ are exchanged and
$f_3$ is fixed. For the second, substitution in
\eqref{eq:bkm-square-2-reflection} exchanges $2f_2-f_3$ and $f_3$
and fixes $2f_{-2}-f_3$. The third follows by exchanging $f_2$
and $f_{-2}$. Each permutation preserves $A$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Weyl vector]
\label{prop:bkm-weyl-vector}
The equations
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bkm-weyl-vector-condition}
 (\rho,\delta_i)=-\tfrac12\delta_i^2=-1
\end{equation}
have the unique solution
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bkm-weyl-vector}
 \rho=\tfrac12(\delta_1+\delta_2+\delta_3)
 =f_2-\tfrac12f_3+f_{-2}\in L^*\cap V_+,
 \qquad \rho^2=-\tfrac32.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The matrix $A$ is invertible and
$A(1,1,1)^T=(-2,-2,-2)^T$. This determines the coefficients of
$\rho$. Its norm follows from~\eqref{eq:bkm-lambda21-form}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[The fixed line]
\label{rem:bkm-rho-s3-invariant}
The chamber action fixes $\rho$. Its fixed subspace in $L_\Q$ is
exactly $\Q\rho$, since it permutes the three basis vectors
$\delta_i$. In particular, the factor $1/2$ in the Weyl vector is
fixed by~\eqref{eq:bkm-weyl-vector-condition}. The sum of the three
roots has pairing $-2$ with each wall.
\end{remark}

\begin{lemma}[Isotropic chamber rays]
\label{lem:bkm-isotropic-orbit}
The only primitive isotropic rays of $\mathcal C\cap L$ are
\[
 a_{13}=\delta_1+\delta_3=2f_2,\qquad
 a_{23}=\delta_2+\delta_3=2f_{-2},\qquad
 a_{12}=\delta_1+\delta_2=2f_2-2f_3+2f_{-2}.
\]
The chamber group permutes them transitively.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
In the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:bkm-chamber-dual-cone}, a chamber
vector is isotropic precisely when $\sum_{i<j}t_it_j=0$.
For nonnegative $t_i$, this means that at most one is nonzero.
The resulting three rays have the displayed primitive integral
generators. The $S_3$ action permutes the pairs of indices.
\end{proof}

\section{From Fourier coefficients to simple generators}
\label{sec:bkm-roots}
\label{sec:bkm-active-support}

The Fourier coefficients of the weak Jacobi form are denoted by
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bkm-phi01-expansion}
 \phi_{0,1}(\tau,z)=\sum_{n\geq0,\ l\in\Z}f(n,l)q^nr^l
 =r^{-1}+10+r+O(q).
\end{equation}
Elliptic translation and evenness make $f(n,l)$ a function of
$4n-l^2$. It vanishes for $4n-l^2<-1$. Let $\Gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}$
be the cusp support
\[
 \{m>0,n\geq0,l\in\Z\}\ \cup\
 \{m=0,n>0,l\in\Z\}\ \cup\
 \{m=n=0,l<0\}\subset\Z^3.
\]

\begin{definition}[Active support]
\label{def:bkm-active-support}
Set
\[
 \Gamma_{\mathrm{act}}=\{(n,l,m)\in\Gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}:f(nm,l)\ne0\},
 \qquad \Cone_{\mathrm{Borch}}=\R_{\geq0}\alpha(\Gamma_{\mathrm{act}}).
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[Positive root coordinates]
\label{lem:bkm-active-cone}
Every active degree has
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bkm-active-decomposition}
 \alpha(n,l,m)=n\delta_1+m\delta_2+(n+m-l)\delta_3\in Q_+\setminus\{0\}.
\end{equation}
Moreover $\Cone_{\mathrm{Borch}}=\Delta(L)_+$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The first two coefficients are nonnegative. Activity implies
$l^2\leq4nm+1$. If $n,m>0$ and $l\geq n+m+1$, then
$l^2\geq4nm+(n-m)^2+2(n+m)+1>4nm+1$. The cases with $nm=0$
follow from $|l|\leq1$ and the cusp convention. Thus $n+m-l\geq0$.
The active triples $(1,1,0),(0,1,1),(0,-1,0)$ give the three
simple vectors, proving equality of the cones.
\end{proof}

For $\beta=\sum b_i\delta_i\in Q_+$ define its height by
$\operatorname{ht}\beta=b_1+b_2+b_3$. There are finitely many
lattice degrees below any fixed height. We use the corresponding
completion $\Z[[Q_+]]$: its elements are formal sums
$\sum_{\beta\in Q_+}a_\beta X^\beta$, and multiplication is defined
by the finite sums over $\beta=\beta_1+\beta_2$. Product factors
below always have positive height. This specifies a common formal
domain before any infinite product is formed.

\begin{lemma}[Leading coefficient]
\label{lem:bkm-vacuum-coefficient}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bkm-vacuum-coefficient}
 [q^{1/2}r^{1/2}s^{1/2}]\Delta_5=64.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The ten-theta product gives the leading term
$64q^{1/2}s^{1/2}(r^{1/2}-r^{-1/2})$ by
Proposition~\ref{prop:view1-theta-constant-leading-coefficient}.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Borcherds product]
\label{thm:bkm-borcherds-product-D5}
In the fixed cusp expansion,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bkm-D5-borcherds-product}
 D_5(Z):=64^{-1}\Delta_5(Z)
 =q^{1/2}r^{1/2}s^{1/2}
   \prod_{(n,l,m)\in\Gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}}(1-q^nr^ls^m)^{f(nm,l)}.
\end{equation}
Here $q^nr^ls^m=e^{-\pi i(\alpha(n,l,m),z)}$ and
$q^{1/2}r^{1/2}s^{1/2}=e^{-\pi i(\rho,z)}$ in the tube
coordinates of Chapter~\ref{ch:view1-automorphic}.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
This is the multiplicative lift of $\phi_{0,1}$
\cite[Theorem~2.1 and Example~2.4]{GNII}, equivalently the
orthogonal Borcherds lift on $\La^{3,2}$
\cite[Theorem~13.3]{B}. Lemma~\ref{lem:bkm-active-cone} places
its nontrivial factors in the height completion. The boundary factor
$(1-r^{-1})$ has degree $\delta_3$. The leading coefficient fixes
the normalization.
\end{proof}

The chamber expansion of this product specifies the imaginary
simple generators. It is convenient to use $W=W_{II}$ and to include
the isotropic boundary in $\mathcal C\setminus\{0\}$.

\begin{theorem}[Chamber Fourier correction]
\label{thm:bkm-weyl-alternating-sum}
There are unique integers $m(a)$, indexed by
$a\in(\mathcal C\cap L)\setminus\{0\}$, such that
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bkm-weyl-sum-Delta5}
 D_5(Z)=\sum_{w\in W}\det(w)
 \left(e^{-\pi i(w\rho,z)}-
       \sum_{a\in(\mathcal C\cap L)\setminus\{0\}}
         m(a)e^{-\pi i(w(\rho+a),z)}\right).
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Write $D_5=\sum_\lambda c_\lambda e^{-\pi i(\lambda,z)}$ with
$\lambda\in\rho+L$. Its nonzero Fourier coefficients lie in $V_+$
by the cusp condition. The linear reflection law from
Lemma~\ref{lem:view1-maass-multiplier-restriction} gives
$c_{w\lambda}=\det(w)c_\lambda$. A coefficient on a reflecting
wall vanishes.

Each surviving orbit therefore has a unique
representative in the chamber interior. For this representative
$\lambda=\rho+a$, the pairings $(a,\delta_i)$ are even integers,
so $(\lambda,\delta_i)<0$ implies $(a,\delta_i)\leq0$.
Lemma~\ref{lem:bkm-chamber-dual-cone} puts $a$ in $\mathcal C$.

The coefficient at $\rho$ is one. Define $m(a)=-c_{\rho+a}$ for
$a\ne0$ and sum the alternating orbits. This is
\cite[Theorem~2.3(a)]{GN}. The chamber condition concerns the orbit
representative: $s_{\delta_i}\rho=\rho+\delta_i$ is another
nonzero Fourier term, although $\delta_i^2=2$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Null-ray correction]
\label{lem:bkm-isotropic-eta-product}
For any of the three primitive null vectors $a=a_{ij}$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bkm-isotropic-tau}
 1-\sum_{n\geq1}m(na)t^n
 =\prod_{n\geq1}(1-t^n)^9,
 \qquad \tau(na):=9.
\end{equation}
For $a=2f_2$, the left side is also
$1+64^{-1}\sum_{n\geq1}c_\Delta(1+2n,1,1)t^n$, where
$c_\Delta(u,v,w)$ denotes the coefficient of $q^{u/2}r^{v/2}s^{w/2}$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The first Fourier--Jacobi coefficient of $\Delta_5$ is the lift
input $\eta^9\vartheta$. Its restriction along this ray gives
\eqref{eq:bkm-isotropic-tau} \cite[Section~3]{GN}.
The chamber permutations carry it to the other two rays.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Imaginary simple generators]
\label{def:bkm-imaginary-simple-roots}
At a timelike $a\in\mathcal C\cap L$, prescribe
\[
 \mu_{\overline0}(a)=\max(m(a),0),\qquad
 \mu_{\overline1}(a)=\max(-m(a),0).
\]
At every nonzero multiple of a primitive chamber null vector,
prescribe $\mu_{\overline0}(na_{ij})=9$ and
$\mu_{\overline1}(na_{ij})=0$. All other imaginary simple fibers
are zero. Denote this multiset by $\Delta^{im}_{\mathrm{simp}}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Simple parity and root parity]
\label{rem:bkm-parity-split}
The signs of $m(a)$ determine the parity of the timelike
\emph{simple} generators in this presentation. A full root space also
contains brackets of lower degrees. Its product exponent is the difference of its even and odd dimensions.
The sign of that exponent does not determine the parity of every vector.
At $a_{ij}$, nine simple vectors and one real-real bracket give ten
even vectors. At $\delta_1+\delta_2+\delta_3$, both parities occur.
\end{remark}

\section{The Borcherds presentation}
\label{sec:bkm-generators-relations}
\label{sec:bkm-structural-properties}

\begin{lemma}[Cartan inequalities]
\label{lem:bkm-cartan-conditions}
Distinct simple vectors have nonpositive pairing. Real simple
vectors have square two, imaginary simple vectors have nonpositive
square, and $2(\delta_i,a)/\delta_i^2$ is integral for every simple
vector $a$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The real pairings are the off-diagonal entries of $A$.
The chamber inequalities give $(a,\delta_i)\leq0$ for imaginary
$a$. Two vectors in the same closed future cone have nonpositive
pairing. Integrality follows from the integral form on $L$.
Repeated copies of a null vector have pairing zero. Repeated copies
of a timelike vector have negative pairing.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Borcherds--Cartan superdatum]
\label{def:bkm-borcherds-cartan-superdatum}
Let $I$ index one even generator at each $\delta_i$, and
$\mu_{\bar p}(a)$ generators of parity $\bar p$ at every imaginary
simple degree $a$. Denote their degree and parity maps by
$i\mapsto\alpha_i\in L$ and $i\mapsto p(i)\in\Z/2$.
Together with the even Cartan space $\mathfrak h=L\otimes\C$
and its form, these data define $\mathscr B_{\Delta_5}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Denominator algebra]
\label{def:bkm-relations}
The Lie superalgebra $\autcor=\mathfrak g$ is generated by
$\mathfrak h$ and $e_i,f_i$ for $i\in I$, with
$|e_i|=|f_i|=p(i)$ and degrees $\alpha_i,-\alpha_i$.
Identify $h_i=\alpha_i$ in $\mathfrak h$. Impose
\begin{align}
\label{eq:bkm-cartan-relations}
 [h,h']&=0,&[h,e_j]&=(h,\alpha_j)e_j,&
 [h,f_j]&=-(h,\alpha_j)f_j,\\
\label{eq:bkm-chevalley-relations}
 [e_i,f_j]&=\delta_{ij}h_i.
\end{align}
For a real simple index $i$ and $j\ne i$, impose
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bkm-real-serre}
 (\operatorname{ad}e_i)^{1-2(\alpha_i,\alpha_j)/\alpha_i^2}e_j=0,
 \qquad
 (\operatorname{ad}f_i)^{1-2(\alpha_i,\alpha_j)/\alpha_i^2}f_j=0.
\end{equation}

Finally, impose
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bkm-orthogonality}
 [e_i,e_j]=[f_i,f_j]=0\quad\text{if }(\alpha_i,\alpha_j)=0.
\end{equation}
All brackets satisfy super-antisymmetry and the super Jacobi
identity. For homogeneous vectors,
\[
 [x,y]=-(-1)^{|x||y|}[y,x].
\]
\end{definition}

These are the symmetrizable generalized Kac--Moody relations without
odd real simple roots \cite[Statement~6.4 and equations~(6.26)--(6.30)]{GN}.
They can be obtained from the derived contragredient algebra with
one independent Cartan generator for each $i\in I$ by quotienting
its central Cartan kernel under $h_i\mapsto\alpha_i$.
The specified Cartan space is nondegenerate and spanned by the
three real vectors.

\begin{remark}[Real Serre exponent]
\label{rem:bkm-real-serre-exponent}
For distinct real indices, $1-2(\delta_i,\delta_j)/\delta_i^2=3$.
Thus $(\operatorname{ad}e_i)^3e_j=0$, while $[e_i,e_j]$ survives.
The matrix $A/2$ is a rescaled bilinear form, not the generalized
Cartan matrix in this normalization: real diagonal entries of that
matrix are two.
\end{remark}

\begin{lemma}[Triangular decomposition and local finiteness]
\label{lem:bkm-triangular-decomposition}
There is a decomposition
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bkm-triangular}
 \mathfrak g=\mathfrak n_-\oplus\mathfrak h\oplus\mathfrak n_+,
 \qquad
 \mathfrak n_\pm=\bigoplus_{\beta\in Q_+\setminus\{0\}}
                         \mathfrak g_{\pm\beta},
\end{equation}
with $\mathfrak g_0=\mathfrak h$ and
$[\mathfrak g_\beta,\mathfrak g_\gamma]
\subset\mathfrak g_{\beta+\gamma}$. Every homogeneous root space is
finite-dimensional. More strongly, the sum of its dimensions over
$0<\operatorname{ht}\beta\leq N$ is finite for every $N$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The triangular decomposition follows from the symmetrizable
presentation theorem just cited. For its grading, first use the free
abelian group $\widetilde Q=\bigoplus_{i\in I}\Z r_i$, then send
$r_i$ to $\alpha_i$. Every $\alpha_i$ has positive height. Only
finitely many simple degrees have height at most $N$, and each has
finite multiplicity. A positive word of total height at most $N$
has length at most $N$ and uses only this finite set of generators.
There are therefore finitely many such words.

This proves the
finite-fiber condition of \cite[(6.29)--(6.30)]{GN} and the asserted
finiteness after passing to relations. No nonempty positive word
has degree zero.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Root sets and signed multiplicity]
\label{def:bkm-full-root-datum}
\label{def:bkm-real-roots}
The positive roots are
$\mathcal R_+=\{\beta\in Q_+\setminus\{0\}:\mathfrak g_\beta\ne0\}$.
The negative roots are $-\mathcal R_+$. The real roots form
$\Delta^{re}=W\{\delta_1,\delta_2,\delta_3\}$, and all remaining
roots are imaginary. The imaginary root spaces are the homogeneous
pieces of the presentation, including all brackets that survive its
relations. They are not just Weyl translates of the simple fibers.
\label{def:bkm-smult}
For every lattice degree set
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bkm-smult-def}
 e_\beta=\dim\mathfrak g_{\beta,\overline0},\qquad
 o_\beta=\dim\mathfrak g_{\beta,\overline1},\qquad
 \smult(\beta)=e_\beta-o_\beta.
\end{equation}
We also use $\rootmult_{\bar p}(\beta)$ for the two dimensions.
\end{definition}

For a real root $\beta$, write
$\Hpl_\beta=\{\R_{>0}x:x\in V_+,\ (x,\beta)=0\}$.
The three simple mirrors bound $\Poly_{II}$. Their Weyl translates
form $\bigcup_{\beta\in\Delta^{re}}\Hpl_\beta$.
The type-I chamber reflections permute the three simple mirrors.
A physical wall interpretation additionally requires a comparison
with the geometric parameter space.

The chosen cusp also splits the positive half. For
$\beta=\alpha(n,l,m)$, let $c(\beta)=m$, the second simple-root
coordinate. Then $\mathfrak n_+=\mathfrak n_+^{>0}\oplus\mathfrak n_+^0$,
where
\begin{align*}
 \mathfrak n_+^{>0}&=\bigoplus_{\beta\in\mathcal R_+,\ c(\beta)>0}
                      \mathfrak g_\beta,\\
 \mathfrak n_+^0&=\bigoplus_{\beta\in\mathcal R_+,\ c(\beta)=0}
                      \mathfrak g_\beta.
\end{align*}

The second summand is supported on the cusp boundary.
They do not split the Cartan or negative half.
Additivity of $c$ makes the first an ideal of $\mathfrak n_+$
and the second a subalgebra. This cusp degree is distinct from
the chamber multiplicity $m(a)$.

\begin{lemma}[Real subalgebra]
\label{lem:bkm-real-sub-km}
The real generators and $\mathfrak h$ generate the Kac--Moody
algebra $\bkm(A)$. Its inclusion in $\mathfrak g$ is injective.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
In the positive presentation, every relation involving an imaginary
index has positive degree in that index for the finer
$\widetilde Q$-grading. Hence it imposes no relation on the purely
real words. The remaining positive relations are exactly the real
Serre relations. The same argument applies to the negative half.
Together with the injective Cartan identification and triangular
decomposition, this proves injectivity of the full real subalgebra.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Invariant bilinear form]
\label{lem:bkm-invariant-form}
There is a unique even nondegenerate invariant supersymmetric form
$B_{\mathfrak g}$ extending the form on $\mathfrak h$. It satisfies
\[
 B_{\mathfrak g}(e_i,f_j)=\delta_{ij},\qquad
 B_{\mathfrak g}([x,y],z)=B_{\mathfrak g}(x,[y,z]).
\]
Root spaces pair only in opposite degrees, and their opposite-degree
pairings are perfect.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Use the invariant-form theorem for symmetrizable contragredient
superalgebras \cite[Chapter~2]{KAC:1}, with the super modification
and central quotient of \cite[Section~6, Type~1 and~(6.28)]{GN}.
The Cartan form here is nondegenerate, so after that quotient no
Cartan radical remains. The theorem gives nondegeneracy and
invariance.

Its normalization can also be read directly:
\[
 B_{\mathfrak g}([e_i,f_j],h)
 =B_{\mathfrak g}(e_i,[f_j,h])
 =(\alpha_j,h)B_{\mathfrak g}(e_i,f_j).
\]
The left side is $\delta_{ij}(\alpha_i,h)$. Since $\alpha_j\ne0$
and the Cartan form is nondegenerate, this forces the stated value.
Invariance recursively determines the pairings of bracket words.

For weights $\beta,\gamma$, invariance under $h$ gives
$(h,\beta+\gamma)B_{\mathfrak g}(x_\beta,y_\gamma)=0$.
Thus only opposite weights pair. Local finiteness then turns the
nondegenerate opposite pairing into a perfect pairing.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Even Weyl transport]
\label{lem:bkm-weyl-transport-parity}
The operators
$\exp(\operatorname{ad}e_i)\exp(-\operatorname{ad}f_i)
 \exp(\operatorname{ad}e_i)$ at real simple indices induce even
isomorphisms
$\mathfrak g_{\beta,\bar p}\simeq\mathfrak g_{s_i\beta,\bar p}$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The Serre relations give local nilpotence of the real adjoint
actions. These exponentials therefore define the usual Weyl
operators \cite[Chapter~3]{KAC:1}. The generators are even, so the
operators preserve parity \cite[Section~6]{GN}.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Real roots and simple imaginary fibers]
\label{lem:bkm-real-root-multiplicity-one}
Every real root has parity dimensions $1|0$.
\label{lem:bkm-imaginary-simple-fibre}
At an imaginary simple degree $a$, let $E_a^{\mathrm{simp}}$ be
the span of its specified generators. Its signed dimension is
$\mu_{\overline0}(a)-\mu_{\overline1}(a)$. The full root space
may contain further bracket vectors.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The assertions hold at the simple generators by the positive
presentation. Weyl transport proves the real-root assertion on
the entire real orbit. In the fine positive grading, simple
generators and decomposable words have distinct degrees, even when
their images in $L$ coincide.
\end{proof}

\section{Two denominator identities}
\label{sec:bkm-denominator-identity}
\label{sec:bkm-denominator-consistency}

For the following formulas let $X^\beta=e^{-2\pi i(\beta,z)}$.
$X^\rho\Z[[Q_+]]$ denotes the shifted height-completed module.
Define the chamber correction series
\begin{align*}
 S_s&=1-\sum_{a\in(\mathcal C\cap L)\setminus\{0\}}m(a)X^a,\\
 S_o&=1-\sum_{\substack{a\in\mathcal C\cap L\\a^2<0}}|m(a)|X^a
       +\sum_{i<j}\left(\prod_{n\geq1}(1-X^{na_{ij}})^9-1\right).
\end{align*}
The null-ray terms in $S_o$ retain their eta-product coefficients.
Only the timelike coefficients undergo the absolute-value operation.

\begin{theorem}[Superdenominator and ordinary denominator]
\label{thm:bkm-denominator-identity}
The algebra $\mathfrak g$ satisfies
\begin{align}
\label{eq:bkm-denominator-identity}
 X^\rho D_s
 &:=\sum_{w\in W}\det(w)X^{w\rho}w(S_s)
   =64^{-1}\Delta_5(2Z),\\
\label{eq:bkm-denominator-product}
 D_s&=\prod_{\beta\in Q_+\setminus\{0\}}
                  (1-X^\beta)^{e_\beta-o_\beta},\\
\label{eq:bkm-ordinary-denominator}
 D_o&:=X^{-\rho}\sum_{w\in W}\det(w)X^{w\rho}w(S_o)
   =\prod_{\beta\in Q_+\setminus\{0\}}
           (1-X^\beta)^{e_\beta}(1+X^\beta)^{-o_\beta}.
\end{align}
In particular,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bkm-denominator-equality}
 \operatorname{den}(\mathfrak g):=X^\rho D_s
 =64^{-1}\Delta_5(2Z),
\end{equation}
and, for every $\beta=\alpha(n,l,m)\in Q_+\setminus\{0\}$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bkm-active-multiplicities}
 e_\beta-o_\beta=f(nm,l).
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Apply the two superalgebra denominator identities
\cite[Statements~6.7$'$--6.8$'$]{GN} to the presentation.
In the super identity, the imaginary correction is $S_s$.
For the ordinary identity, each timelike simple generator
contributes minus its ordinary multiplicity. Two timelike future
vectors cannot be orthogonal, nor can a timelike vector be
orthogonal to a nonzero future null vector. Orthogonal collections
of imaginary generators therefore consist either of one timelike
generator or of generators on one null ray.

All null generators are
even, so these collections give the three eta products in $S_o$.
This proves both product formulas.

The Weyl sums are locally finite in height. For a reduced word $w$,
$w\rho-\rho$ is the sum of its positive inversion roots, hence has
height at least the word length. Also $wa-a\in Q_+$ for
$a\in\mathcal C$. Only finitely many Weyl words and chamber degrees
can therefore contribute to a fixed height. The positive root spaces
are finite by Lemma~\ref{lem:bkm-triangular-decomposition}.

The chamber Fourier formula with $z$ replaced by $2z$ gives the
first equality with $\Delta_5(2Z)$. Compare its product with
\eqref{eq:bkm-D5-borcherds-product}. Euler factors in $\Z[[Q_+]]$
are unique: remove them successively in increasing height, where
the first nonconstant term of $(1-X^\beta)^a$ is $-aX^\beta$.
Thus the exponents agree, including zero exponents.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Exponential normalization]
\label{rem:bkm-doubling-Z}
The substitution $Z\mapsto2Z$ sends $q,r,s$ to $q^2,r^2,s^2$
and $e^{-\pi i(\beta,z)}$ to $e^{-2\pi i(\beta,z)}$.
It leaves the integral lattice $L$ and the degree map $\alpha$
unchanged. In particular $L\ne2\La^{2,1}$: its $f_3$ direction
is primitive. The Jacobi form $\phi_{0,1}$ is the multiplicative
input, whereas the additive lift uses $\eta^9\vartheta$.
\label{rem:bkm-doubling-power-of-two}
The constant $64=2^6$ is the leading theta-product coefficient.
The number $2^{12}$ in a stipulated half-Hilbert orientation
model counts a different finite orbit. It does not normalize this
denominator.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[Recovery of both parities]
\label{prop:bkm-all-height-parities}
Write the ordinary denominator uniquely as
$D_o=\prod_{\beta>0}(1-X^\beta)^{b_\beta}$, and put
$f_\beta=f(nm,l)$ when $\beta=\alpha(n,l,m)$.
The parity dimensions of the fixed presentation are determined
recursively by
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bkm-parity-recursion}
 o_\beta=\frac{b_\beta-f_\beta+o_{\beta/2}}2,
 \qquad e_\beta=f_\beta+o_\beta,
\end{equation}
where $o_{\beta/2}=0$ if $\beta/2\notin Q_+\setminus\{0\}$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The identity
$(1+X^\beta)^{-o_\beta}
 =(1-X^\beta)^{o_\beta}(1-X^{2\beta})^{-o_\beta}$ gives
$b_\beta=e_\beta+o_\beta-o_{\beta/2}$.
Combine it with $f_\beta=e_\beta-o_\beta$.
The half-degree has smaller height, so the formula is recursive.
Existence of the presented algebra and its two denominator identities
ensure nonnegative integral results. This argument applies at every
height. It does not infer an infinite assertion from a finite table.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[The first interior degree]
\label{prop:bkm-delta123-presentation-count}

 T_1=[e_1,E_{23}],\qquad T_2=[e_2,E_{31}],\qquad

The multilinear part of the free Lie algebra on three real generators

Each mixed real-null word is also free of a relation at this degree.
The fine generator grading separates the $27$ choices from each
other and from the real words. Thus the even contribution is
$2+27=29$.

The chamber Fourier coefficient is
$m(\delta_{123})=-64^{-1}c_\Delta(3,3,3)=-93$:
the coefficient of $qrs$ in the normalized product is $64+29=93$.

\end{proof}

\section{A differential and its first Serre obstruction}
\label{sec:bkm-root-differential}

The two parities at $\gamma:=\delta_{123}$ permit a complex with
smaller cohomology but the same signed dimension. Whether such a
complex respects the root operations is a further question.
Consider first the genuine positive-height quotient
\[
 L_{\leq3}=\mathfrak n_+/
       \bigoplus_{\operatorname{ht}\beta>3}\mathfrak g_\beta.
\]
Its dimensions by height are $3$, $30$, and $35|93$, respectively,
so its total dimension is $161$. Give its even simple generators
cohomological degree zero and its $93$ odd generators $c^u$ degree
$-1$. Cochain parity supplies the super sign.

\begin{proposition}[Cancellation in the height-three quotient]
\label{prop:bkm-height-three-differential}
Choose a basis $w_1,\ldots,w_{29}$ of
$W_\gamma:=\mathfrak g_{\gamma,\overline0}$.
The assignments
\[
 dc^u=w_u\quad(1\leq u\leq28),\qquad
 dc^u=0\quad(29\leq u\leq93),\qquad de_i=du_{ij,r}=0
\]
define a dg Lie structure on $L_{\leq3}$. At $\gamma$ its
cohomology is $H^{-1}=\C^{65}$ and $H^0=\C$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Every height-three vector is central in this positive quotient.
The assigned derivation therefore respects the relations and
squares to zero. Its degree-$\gamma$ complex is
$\C^{93}\longrightarrow\C^{29}$ with rank $28$.
The pairs $(c^u,w_u)$ for $u\leq28$ are contractible, with homotopy
$w_u\mapsto c^u$. The remaining $65$ odd and one even vector give
the stated cohomology.
\end{proof}

This calculation explains how $29-93=-64$ can coexist with
cohomology dimensions $1|65$. It makes no choice of a geometric
differential. Such a choice would have to arise from the coefficient
complexes and preserve their operations. Even abstractly, the
unchanged Borcherds relations constrain the extension.

\begin{lemma}[Lowering detects the interior even space]
\label{lem:bkm-lowering-detector}
Put $E_i=\operatorname{ad}e_i$, $F_i=\operatorname{ad}f_i$ and
$H_i=\operatorname{ad}h_i$. The map
\[
 W_\gamma\longrightarrow\bigoplus_i\mathfrak g_{\gamma-\delta_i},
 \qquad w\longmapsto(F_1w,F_2w,F_3w)
\]
is injective, and $F_i^2W_\gamma=0$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For the real basis $u=[e_1,[e_2,e_3]]$ and
$v=[e_2,[e_1,e_3]]$, Chevalley and Jacobi give
\[
\begin{array}{c|cc}
 &u&v\\\hline
 F_1&4E_{23}&2E_{23}\\
 F_2&2E_{13}&4E_{13}\\
 F_3&-2E_{12}&2E_{12}
\end{array}
\]
The first two coefficient rows have determinant $12$.

For a null simple vector $b\in\operatorname{span}\{u_{ij,r}\}$,
\[
 F_k[e_k,b]=4b,\qquad F_i[e_k,b]=F_j[e_k,b]=0
 \quad(\{i,j,k\}=\{1,2,3\}).
\]
The nine simple directions at $a_{ij}$ are independent of $E_{ij}$.
These blocks therefore detect all $27$ mixed coefficients. The real
block detects the remaining two. A second lowering has a negative
simple-root coordinate and vanishes.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[First obstruction to extending the differential]
\label{thm:bkm-serre-nonextension}
A root-preserving odd derivation of the unchanged positive
Borcherds algebra vanishes on all $c^u$.
Set the rank-$28$ differential above to zero on all other simple
generators. It descends to every positive-height quotient of height
at most five, and fails at height six.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The purely even real-root spaces force $de_i=0$.
For $w=dc^u\in W_\gamma$, differentiation of the defining relation
$E_i^3c^u=0$ gives $E_i^3w=0$. On $W_\gamma$ we have
$H_iw=-2w$ and $F_i^2w=0$. The $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ commutation
relations give
\[
 F_iE_i^aF_i^bw
 =E_i^aF_i^{b+1}w
   -a(-2-2b+a-1)E_i^{a-1}F_i^bw.
\]

Four successive lowerings of $E_i^3w$ are
$E_i^3F_iw$, $6E_i^2F_iw$, $36E_iF_iw$, and $144F_iw$.
Hence $E_i^3w=0$ implies $F_iw=0$ for every $i$.
Lemma~\ref{lem:bkm-lowering-detector} forces $w=0$.
The negative generators only detect the positive relations. No
extension of $d$ to them is assumed.

The first relations involving $c^u$ are the real Serre relations
at $\gamma+3\delta_i$, of height six. Orthogonality gives none
at lower height: $(\gamma,\delta_i)=-2$ and
$(\gamma,a_{ij})=-4$. Thus through height five every surviving
relation either contains no $c^u$ and is closed, or is absent by
height. The differential preserves height and squares to zero on
generators, so it descends through those quotients. At height six
the injective detector rules out its nonzero image.
\end{proof}

There is a separate restriction on retaining the real algebra in
cohomology. If $R_\gamma$ is its two-dimensional real multilinear
part and $W'\subset W_\gamma$ has dimension $28$, then
\[
 \dim(W'\cap R_\gamma)\geq28+2-29=1.
\]
Thus making $W'$ exact while keeping the real generators closed
kills at least one nonzero real bracket in cohomology.

\begin{proposition}[Free attachment of the desired boundaries]
\label{prop:bkm-free-dg-attachment}
Let $\mathfrak a$ be the ordinary Lie algebra generated by the real
and null simple vectors with their prescribed relations.
Choose $w_1,\ldots,w_{28}\in\mathfrak a_\gamma$ and form
\[
 \mathcal L=\mathfrak a*\operatorname{Lie}(c^1,\ldots,c^{93}),
 \qquad dc^u=\begin{cases}w_u,&u\leq28,\\0,&u>28,\end{cases}
 \qquad d\mathfrak a=0,
\]
where the $c^u$ have degree $-1$ and root $\gamma$.
This is a dg Lie algebra with
$H^0(\mathcal L)=\mathfrak a/\langle w_1,\ldots,w_{28}\rangle_{\mathrm{Lie}}$.
The differential extends to its positive-height completion.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The derivation extends freely and its square is zero on generators.
Degree zero is $\mathfrak a$. Every degree-$-1$ word has one
$c^u$, so its differential is an iterated bracket of $w_u$ with
$\mathfrak a$. These images generate exactly the stated ideal.
Root degree is preserved, giving continuity in positive height.
\end{proof}

This attachment imposes no new Serre relation on the $c^u$.
For example, $d[c^i,c^j]=-[c^j,w_i]-[c^i,w_j]$ records relations
among its boundaries in negative cohomological degrees.
For a homotopy-invariant derived quotient, make the attachment to a
semifree resolution of $\mathfrak a$ using chosen cycle lifts of
the $w_u$. Imposing an additional differential
$dz=E_i^3c^u$ would require $d^2z=E_i^3w_u=0$. The theorem
shows why that requirement fails in the original root algebra.
The construction provides a different derived algebra, whose
comparison with geometric elliptic coefficients remains to be made.

\section{Finite operations and completions}
\label{sec:bkm-finite-operations}

A finite set of roots gives finite-dimensional vector spaces.
It gives a Lie algebra only when the bracket has a suitable
quotient or closure property. The positive-height quotients just
used have this property because positive heights add.

\begin{proposition}[Positive completion]
\label{prop:bkm-positive-completion}
The subspace
\[
 \mathfrak n_{+,>N}
 =\bigoplus_{\operatorname{ht}\beta>N}\mathfrak g_\beta
\]
is a Lie ideal of $\mathfrak n_+$. Its finite-dimensional nilpotent
quotients define the topological Lie superalgebra
\[
 \widehat{\mathfrak n}_+
 =\varprojlim_N\mathfrak n_+/\mathfrak n_{+,>N}
 =\prod_{\beta>0}\mathfrak g_\beta.
\]
The bracket projected to
$\mathfrak h\oplus\bigoplus_i(\mathfrak g_{\delta_i}
\oplus\mathfrak g_{-\delta_i})$ fails Jacobi.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
A bracket of positive degrees has their sum as degree, so the
specified subspace is an ideal. An iterated bracket of more than
$N$ positive inputs has height greater than $N$. The quotient is
therefore nilpotent and finite-dimensional. Every coefficient of
the completed bracket is a finite sum, giving continuity.

For the projected full bracket, take $i\ne j$.
The term $[e_i,[e_j,f_j]]=[e_i,h_j]=2e_i$ survives,
whereas the intermediate $[e_i,e_j]$ is projected to zero and
$[e_i,f_j]=0$. Thus the derivation form of Jacobi has defect $2e_i$.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Finite homogeneous operation datum]
\label{def:bkm-finite-target-presentation-datum}
A finite set $W\subset\mathcal R_+$ is \emph{downward saturated} if it
contains every positive root $\beta'\leq\beta\in W$, where
$\beta-\beta'\in Q_+$. Put $S_W=W\cup(-W)\cup\{0\}$.
The target datum contains the spaces $\mathfrak g_{\beta,\bar p}$
for $\beta\in S_W$ and $B_{\mathfrak g}$ on opposite degrees.
It retains each homogeneous bracket map whose input and output
degrees belong to $S_W$. It also specifies the
simple generators, their defining relations, and bases of the
finite enveloping pieces defined below. Every iterated identity
is required in a larger set containing all its intermediate degrees.
\end{definition}

Such a datum is a fragment of the graded algebra. It is not assigned
a bracket that sets omitted outputs to zero. In particular, its
finite matrices describe operations inherited from $\mathfrak g$,
not an enveloping algebra of the fragment itself.

\begin{proposition}[Finite filtered enveloping spaces]
\label{prop:bkm-finite-filtered-pbw}
Choose homogeneous root bases and a basis of $\mathfrak h$.
Give a root basis vector weight $|\operatorname{ht}\beta|$ and a
Cartan basis vector weight one. Ordered super-PBW monomials of total
weight at most $N$ span a finite-dimensional space $F_NU(\mathfrak g)$.
These spaces exhaust $U(\mathfrak g)$ and satisfy
\[
 F_MF_N\subset F_{M+N},\qquad
 \Delta(F_N)\subset\sum_{a+b\leq N}F_a\otimes F_b,\qquad
 S(F_N)\subset F_N.
\]

Here $\Delta(x)=x\otimes1+1\otimes x$ and $S(x)=-x$ on
$\mathfrak g$. The ordinary PBW length filtration is an additional
filtration. Its intersection with each $F_N$ is finite-dimensional.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Only finitely many root and Cartan basis vectors have weight at
most $N$, and each has positive weight. There are finitely many
ordered monomials of bounded total weight, with an odd vector
occurring at most once. Reordering uses
$xy-(-1)^{|x||y|}yx=[x,y]$. The bracket weight is at most the sum
of the input weights, including opposite roots whose bracket lies
in the Cartan. Thus multiplication and the Hopf operations respect
the stated bounds. The super PBW theorem proves that these
monomials form bases.
\end{proof}

A finite filtered piece is generally not an algebra. Already in
$U(\mathfrak{sl}_2)$, order the basis by $f<h<e$ and project each
product to PBW length at most one. The projected product satisfies
$e*f=h$, $h*f=-2f$ and $f*f=0$, so
\[
 (e*f)*f-e*(f*f)=-2f.
\]
The associative multiplication is the map into the larger filtered
piece specified by the proposition.

\begin{theorem}[Reconstruction from finite operations]
\label{thm:bkm-finite-operation-reconstruction}
Let $P=\bigoplus_{\beta\in L}P_\beta$ be a graded Lie superalgebra
with finite-dimensional homogeneous spaces and no support outside
$\mathcal R_+\cup(-\mathcal R_+)\cup\{0\}$. Suppose compatible
even isomorphisms $A_\beta:P_\beta\to\mathfrak g_\beta$ are given
on an exhaustion of all root degrees and the Cartan. If every
homogeneous bracket square commutes in some finite set containing
its inputs and output, then $\bigoplus_\beta A_\beta$ is a graded
Lie isomorphism. If the opposite-degree pairing squares commute,
it also preserves the specified invariant forms. It induces a
filtered Hopf isomorphism of the ordinary enveloping algebras and
an isomorphism of the positive-height Lie completions.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Compatibility defines $A$ on every homogeneous space. Algebraic
elements have finite support. Every pair of such elements is
contained in a finite set of homogeneous bracket squares, so $A$
preserves brackets. The inverses have the same property.

Pairing
compatibility is checked on the finitely many opposite-degree
terms. The enveloping assertion follows by its universal property
and the super PBW theorem. On the positive half, the maps commute
with the genuine height quotients. The compatible inverses induce
the inverse on their limit.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Obstruction to the full root-product topology]
\label{prop:bkm-two-sided-completion-obstruction}
The bracket of $\mathfrak g$ has no jointly continuous extension
to $\mathfrak h\oplus\prod_{\beta\ne0}\mathfrak g_\beta$ with
its coordinatewise linear topology and discrete Cartan factor.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Fix a primitive null ray $a=a_{ij}$ and choose one pair of simple
generators $e_n,f_n$ in degrees $na,-na$ for each $n\geq1$.
Chevalley gives $[e_n,f_m]=\delta_{nm}na$.
The two partial sums $\sum_{n=1}^Ne_n$ and $\sum_{n=1}^Nf_n$
converge coordinatewise, but their brackets have Cartan component
$N(N+1)a/2$, which is not eventually constant. This contradicts
continuity.
\end{proof}

One valid enlargement is
$\mathfrak n_-\oplus\mathfrak h\oplus\widehat{\mathfrak n}_+$,
with the negative and Cartan summands discrete. A negative element
has finite support, so every mixed bracket coefficient is a finite
sum. Its negative output is again finite. Positive-positive
brackets are continuous by height. This defines a topological Lie
algebra. A completion allowing both infinite signs needs a
different summation domain and a new continuity argument.

\subsection{Inverse limits of maps and complexes}

A compatible family of literal isomorphisms of inverse systems
induces an isomorphism on inverse limits: the compatible inverse
maps provide its inverse. No exactness hypothesis is needed for
that assertion. Exactness becomes relevant when forming quotient
objects or passing from quasi-isomorphisms of complexes to ordinary
limits.

\begin{proposition}[Finite towers]
\label{prop:bkm-finite-tower-ml}
Every countable inverse system of finite-dimensional vector spaces
is Mittag--Leffler. Consequently, for a compatible short exact
sequence $0\to K_N\to V_N\to Q_N\to0$ with each $K_N$
finite-dimensional, its inverse limit is exact.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
At a fixed index $N$, the images of later terms in $K_N$ form a
descending chain of subspaces. Their dimensions stabilize, hence
so do the subspaces. This is the Mittag--Leffler condition and
implies $R^1\varprojlim K_N=0$ for a countable tower
\cite[Section~3.5]{WeibelHA}. Apply the inverse-limit exact sequence.
\end{proof}

The assertion applies to primitive spaces, pairing kernels and finite
PBW pieces only after those spaces and their transition maps exist.
Kernels themselves commute with limits of an actual commuting
inverse-system map.

Dualization reverses arrows: if
$T_N:V_{N+1}\to V_N$, then $T_N^*:V_N^*\to V_{N+1}^*$.
Thus finite perfect pairings do not, by themselves, define an
inverse tower of dual spaces. For a surjective inverse tower of
finite-dimensional spaces, the continuous dual of its limit is
$\varinjlim V_N^*$. Comparison with a second inverse limit requires
specified adjoint transition maps. Over a general coefficient ring,
finite generation alone also does not imply the descending-chain
argument used above.

\begin{proposition}[Ordinary limits of complexes]
\label{prop:bkm-limit-complexes}
For a countable tower of cochain complexes, degreewise
Mittag--Leffler chain groups suffice to identify its ordinary
inverse limit with its homotopy inverse limit. Without such a
condition, ordinary inverse limits need not preserve
quasi-isomorphisms, even for towers of acyclic complexes.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $T$ shift a product of the chain groups using the transition
maps. The homotopy inverse limit is
$\operatorname{Cone}(1-T:\prod C_N\to\prod C_N)[-1]$.
Degreewise Mittag--Leffler makes $1-T$ degreewise surjective.
Its kernel complex, the ordinary limit, is then quasi-isomorphic
to that homotopy fiber. Homotopy limits preserve equivalences.

For failure without this hypothesis, take the acyclic complexes
\[
 C_N=\bigl(t^N\C[t]\lhook\joinrel\longrightarrow
             \C[t]\longrightarrow\C[t]/(t^N)\bigr)
\]
in degrees $-1,0,1$. The transition maps are inclusion, identity
and reduction, respectively. Their ordinary limit is
$0\to\C[t]\to\C[[t]]$, whose degree-one cohomology is
$\C[[t]]/\C[t]$. The compatible residues
$1+t+\cdots+t^{N-1}$ have no polynomial lift: such a lift would
have coefficient one in every degree. The infinite-dimensional
tail spaces here are not covered by the preceding proposition.
\end{proof}

\section{Information in the superdenominator}
\label{sec:bkm-chiral-shadow}
\label{sec:bkm-chiral-shadow-character}

\begin{lemma}[Visible and zero exponents]
\label{lem:bkm-active-multiplicity}
On $\Gamma_{\mathrm{act}}$, the signed root dimension is
$\smult(\alpha(n,l,m))=f(nm,l)$. A zero exponent can arise from an absent root space or from equal,
nonzero parity dimensions. The superdenominator alone does not
distinguish these possibilities.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The equality is~\eqref{eq:bkm-active-multiplicities}.
A factor with exponent zero is one for either of the stated parity
possibilities. The ordinary denominator of the fixed presentation
resolves that ambiguity by Proposition~\ref{prop:bkm-all-height-parities}.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Information in a superdenominator]
\label{prop:bkm-information-content}
A product $\prod_{\beta>0}(1-X^\beta)^{f_\beta}$ in the positive
height completion determines every signed exponent $f_\beta$.
Taken without a presentation or ordinary denominator, it determines
neither the two parity dimensions nor a bracket or invariant form.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Taking logarithms over $\Q$ gives
\[
 \log D_s=-\sum_{\beta>0}\sum_{r\geq1}
                 \frac{f_\beta}{r}X^{r\beta}.
\]
At each height the already known smaller degrees can be subtracted,
leaving $f_\beta$. But adjoining an even-odd pair in any degree
does not change $f_\beta$. On fixed graded spaces, the abelian
bracket also gives the same determinant. It differs from the
Borcherds bracket, for which $[e_i,f_i]=h_i\ne0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[The super Grothendieck quotient]
\label{prop:bkm-determinant-grothendieck}
Let $\mathcal B$ be a locally finite super vector space graded by
the positive monoid, with $\sdim\mathcal B_{\alpha(n,l,m)}=f(nm,l)$.
Its completed superdeterminant gives
\[
 64q^{1/2}r^{1/2}s^{1/2}
 \prod_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}}(1-q^nr^ls^m)^{\sdim\mathcal B_{\alpha(n,l,m)}}
 =\Delta_5(Z).
\]
This invariant factors through the quotient of the completed
Grothendieck group by $[\Pi V]=-[V]$, where $\Pi$ reverses parity.
The ordinary Grothendieck group of super vector spaces retains both
parity dimensions.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
In each degree, ordinary $K_0$ is $\Z[\C_{\overline0}]
\oplus\Z[\C_{\overline1}]$. Superdimension is the map
$(e,o)\mapsto e-o$, whose kernel is generated by $(1,1)$.
The superdeterminant is $(1-X^\beta)^{e-o}$, so it factors through
that quotient. Local finiteness makes the product meaningful in
height. The specified signed dimensions identify it with
\eqref{eq:bkm-D5-borcherds-product}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[The extra datum in parity recovery]
\label{rem:bkm-determinant-forgets}
The pair $29|93$ uses the minimal simple-generator prescription
and the Borcherds relations. The superdenominator by itself admits
all pairs $(a,a+64)$. Formula~\eqref{eq:bkm-parity-recursion} recovers
the actual pair because its second input, $D_o$, is constructed
from that prescription. This does not reconstruct a compact Hall
object from a numerical product.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Cusp and root coordinates]
\label{rem:bkm-two-polarizations}
The cusp notation uses $(n,l,m)$ and the root notation uses
$(b_1,b_2,b_3)=(n,m,n+m-l)$. Their common active monomials lie in
$Q_+$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:bkm-active-cone}. The cusp order and the
chosen Weyl chamber specify expansions of the same lattice
characters through $\alpha$. This coordinate identification is an
integral map. It does not supply a duality of coefficient objects.

For a compact source, the normal-ordered charge homomorphism
$\overline\Pi_X$ must still intertwine its operations with this
grading. The quadratic defect of an uncorrected charge assignment
is not an extra term in the target Cartan form.
\end{remark}

\section{The invariant form and primitive recognition}
\label{sec:bkm-primitive-comparison}

If an associative super-bialgebra $H$ is given, its primitives are
$P(H)=\{x:\Delta x=x\otimes1+1\otimes x\}$.
Compatibility of product and coproduct makes them a Lie
superalgebra under the supercommutator. Recognition of
$\mathfrak g$ in such a source requires a Lie form on the primitives.
That form must be distinguished from a pairing adjoint to the
ordinary Hopf product and coproduct.

\begin{proposition}[Hopf adjointness on primitives]
\label{prop:bkm-hopf-pairing-obstruction}
Let $B$ be an even Hopf pairing on a super-bialgebra over $\C$,
with $B(xy,z)=B(x\otimes y,\Delta z)$ and the unit-counit
identities. Then $B([P(H),P(H)],P(H))=0$.
Its restriction cannot be the nondegenerate invariant form of
$\mathfrak g$. The contradiction already occurs on a real
$\mathfrak{sl}_2$ triple.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For primitive $z$, the right side of the adjointness identity has
one unit factor. If $x,y$ are also primitive, the unit-counit
identities give $B(xy,z)=0$, and similarly for the reversed product,
with the Koszul tensor sign. Thus the primitive commutator pairs
to zero with every primitive. On an even real triple in
$\mathfrak g$,
\[
 [h,e]=2e,\qquad B_{\mathfrak g}(e,f)=1,
 \qquad B_{\mathfrak g}([h,e],f)=2.
\]
This contradicts the asserted Hopf adjointness. Even without a unit
normalization, for even primitives the expression
$B(x,z)B(y,1)+B(x,1)B(y,z)$ is symmetric in $x,y$, giving the
same contradiction after subtraction.
\end{proof}

The obstruction fixes the recipient: ordinary primitives and their
ordinary coproduct. It does not exclude a Hall pairing between
different positive and negative objects, a braided coproduct, or
an independently constructed invariant Lie form. Each such choice
has its own compatibility equations.

\begin{proposition}[Radical quotient from a Lie form]
\label{prop:bkm-lie-radical-quotient}
Let $P$ be a Lie superalgebra with an even invariant form $B_P$,
and let $R$ be its radical. Then $R$ is a Lie ideal. The two-sided
ideal $\langle R\rangle$ in $U(P)$ is a Hopf ideal, and
\[
 U(P)/\langle R\rangle\simeq U(P/R)
\]
as Hopf superalgebras.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Invariance implies $B_P([x,r],y)=0$ for every $r\in R$ and all
homogeneous $x,y$, so $[P,R]\subset R$. In $U(P)$ the elements
of $R$ are primitive. Therefore
$\Delta\langle R\rangle\subset\langle R\rangle\otimes U(P)
+U(P)\otimes\langle R\rangle$, the counit kills this ideal, and
the antipode preserves it. The enveloping universal property gives
the displayed quotient isomorphism.
\end{proof}

For a compact source, first construct $P_X$ and its invariant
form $B_X$. Then construct a root-graded homomorphism
\[
 \mathfrak g\longrightarrow P_X/\operatorname{Rad}B_X
\]
from specified source representatives of the real and imaginary
simple generators. The defining relations, generation and absence
of extra relations must be proved. On finite homogeneous spaces,
these statements can be expressed by bracket, pairing and comparison
matrices as in Theorem~\ref{thm:bkm-finite-operation-reconstruction}.
An arbitrary full-rank integral matrix is insufficient over a
coefficient ring: multiplication by two on $\Z$ has full rank and
is not invertible. Over $\C$, equal dimensions and invertible
comparison matrices do give the required vector-space isomorphisms.

\begin{proposition}[Recognition on all primitives]
\label{prop:bkm-connected-hopf-recognition}
Let $H$ be a connected cocommutative Hopf superalgebra over $\C$.
Here connected means that the reduced coproduct is elementwise
conilpotent. Then the natural map $U(P(H))\to H$ is an isomorphism.
Consequently a morphism between such Hopf superalgebras is an
isomorphism if it is an isomorphism on all primitives.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
In the convolution algebra of endomorphisms of $H$, put
$J=\mathrm{id}-\eta\varepsilon$ and
\[
 e=\log^*(\mathrm{id})
   =\sum_{n\geq1}\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}nJ^{*n}.
\]
Every sum is finite on an element by conilpotence. In
$\operatorname{Hom}(H,H\otimes H)$, the maps $x\mapsto x\otimes1$
and $x\mapsto1\otimes x$ commute for convolution by
cocommutativity, and their product is $\Delta$. Taking logarithms
therefore gives $\Delta e(x)=e(x)\otimes1+1\otimes e(x)$.
Thus $e$ takes values in $P(H)$. The identity
$\exp^*(e)=\mathrm{id}$ expresses each element as a finite sum
of products of these primitives, proving surjectivity.

For injectivity, suppose the kernel has a nonzero element of least
PBW filtration degree $d$. Its counit is zero, and its reduced
coproduct lies in the tensor square of filtration degree at most
$d-1$. The map is injective on that smaller filtration, so the
reduced coproduct must vanish.

By super PBW in characteristic zero,
the primitives of $U(P(H))$ are exactly $P(H)$. Indeed, in the
associated graded symmetric coalgebra the coproduct component of
lengths $(1,d-1)$, followed by multiplication, is $d$ times the
identity in length $d$. Thus a primitive has length at most one.
This contradicts injectivity on $P(H)$.
The last assertion follows by functoriality of the enveloping map.
\end{proof}

Agreement on a chosen list of root generators has no such force
without these recipient and generation hypotheses. An isomorphism
of global primitive spaces also does not identify a sheaf of chiral
coefficients without a comparison of its local operations.

\section{Current algebras and their coefficients}
\label{sec:bkm-source-target}

The root algebra determines a chiral algebra on a smooth complex
curve $C$. This construction supplies a concrete comparison object.
Its local state spaces include current descendants absent from the
root denominator.

\begin{definition}[Current chiral envelope]
\label{def:bkm-formal-current-envelope}
Let $\mathcal D_C$ be the sheaf of differential operators on $C$.
The induced right differential module
\[
 \operatorname{Cur}_C(\mathfrak g)=\mathfrak g\otimes_\C\mathcal D_C
\]
has the current Lie-star bracket induced by $[a,b]_{\mathfrak g}$.
Here a Lie-star bracket is a differential-module map to the
small-diagonal direct image, with antisymmetry and Jacobi.
Its level-zero Beilinson--Drinfeld chiral envelope is
\[
 V_C=\mathsf{Den}_{\Delta_5,C}
 :=U_C^{\mathrm{ch}}\operatorname{Cur}_C(\mathfrak g).
\]
A chiral bracket extends the Lie-star bracket to inputs on the
complement of the diagonal, with the corresponding differential-module
and Jacobi conditions. The envelope has the local vacuum map
$\omega_C\to V_C$.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Local current realization]
\label{prop:bkm-formal-current-envelope}
The uncompleted ind differential-module envelope $V_C$ exists.
On a coordinate disk its vacuum module is the level-zero induced
module
\[
 U(\mathfrak g[t,t^{-1}])
       \otimes_{U(\mathfrak g[t])}\C,
\]
and its PBW vector space is
$\operatorname{Sym}_{\mathrm{super}}
 (\mathfrak g\otimes t^{-1}\C[t^{-1}])$.
The zero product of the current states recovers $[a,b]_{\mathfrak g}$.
Its root superdenominator is $64^{-1}\Delta_5(2Z)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The induced current Lie-star module is
\cite[Section~2.5.6(b)(ii)]{BD}. The chiral enveloping construction
and its PBW theorem are \cite[Sections~3.7.1 and~3.7.14]{BD}.
The induced underlying $\mathcal O_C$-module is flat.
Locally, the current operator product is
$a(z)b(w)\sim[a,b](w)/(z-w)$, with no central double pole at level
zero.

PBW orders the negative loop modes and gives the displayed
vacuum module \cite{KACVA}. Thus the first current states recover
the original bracket by their zero product. Apply
Theorem~\ref{thm:bkm-denominator-identity} to that root algebra.
\end{proof}

The last assertion concerns the current bracket, not a character of
the entire vacuum module. Give a mode $a\otimes t^{-r}$ energy
$r$, with formal energy variable $u$. For the positive-root current
envelope, PBW gives the supercharacter
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bkm-current-supercharacter}
 \prod_{\beta>0}\prod_{r\geq1}
           (1-u^rX^\beta)^{-\smult(\beta)}.
\end{equation}
Each fixed root height and energy has finite dimension.

The energy
is the loop-rotation grading. This formula does not require a
Sugawara conformal vector for the infinite-dimensional algebra.
The product includes every descendant mode, so it differs from the
root superdeterminant even before a compact state space is considered.

\begin{proposition}[An infinite current coefficient]
\label{prop:bkm-current-infinite-charge-zero}
For the full two-sided root algebra, the energy-two, root-charge-zero
part of the universal current vacuum module is infinite-dimensional.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
At each $na_{ij}$ choose an even null simple generator and its
negative partner. The PBW monomials
$(e_n\otimes t^{-1})(f_n\otimes t^{-1})|0\rangle$ have energy
two and charge zero. They are independent for all $n\geq1$.
Thus the unpolarized root-and-energy character is not defined by
finite coefficient dimensions. A different convergence prescription
or a separately constructed state is needed to define such a trace.
\end{proof}

\subsection{An associative envelope on Ran space}
\label{sec:bkm-ran-envelope}

An associative bar construction needs an associative recipient,
a tensor product and a unit. We describe one recipient in which
the current chiral algebra has such an envelope.
Let $\mathsf{FinSurj}_+$ be the category of nonempty finite sets
and surjections. Set
\[
 \mathcal R_C=\varprojlim_{\mathsf{FinSurj}_+}\operatorname{DMod}(C^I),
 \qquad \mathcal R_C^+=D(\C)\times\mathcal R_C,
\]
where a surjection $I\twoheadrightarrow J$ acts by exceptional
pullback along its diagonal $C^J\to C^I$.
An object is therefore a compatible family of right differential
modules, together with an isolated empty component.

For an ordered
partition $I=I_1\sqcup I_2$, let $j_{I_1,I_2}$ be the open locus
where points in different blocks are distinct. Chiral convolution is
\[
 (F\star G)_I=
 \bigoplus_{I=I_1\sqcup I_2}
  j_{I_1,I_2,*}j_{I_1,I_2}^{!}(F_{I_1}\boxtimes G_{I_2}).
\]

The empty-block summands use the empty coefficients. The unit $K$
has $K_\varnothing=\C$ and $K_I=0$ for $I\ne\varnothing$.
Call a family positive if its empty component is zero.

For a right differential module $N$ on $C$, its positive
main-diagonal family is $(\iota_*N)_I=\delta_{I,*}N$.
The chiral bracket of $V_C$ makes $\mathcal L_C:=\iota_*V_C$
a Lie object for $\star$. Define the augmented associative envelope
\[
 \mathcal A_{\Delta,C}=U_\star(\mathcal L_C),
 \qquad (\mathcal A_{\Delta,C})_\varnothing=\C.
\]
This algebra and the BD envelope $V_C$ have different carriers.

\begin{proposition}[Bar of the associative envelope]
\label{prop:bkm-ran-bar-envelope}
In $(\mathcal R_C^+,\star,K)$ there are equivalences
\[
 B\mathcal A_{\Delta,C}\simeq
 \mathrm{CE}_\star(\mathcal L_C),\qquad
 \Omega\mathrm{CE}_\star(\mathcal L_C)
       \simeq\mathcal A_{\Delta,C}.
\]
The first coalgebra satisfies factorization. The bar is coaugmented
and counital, its reduced coproduct is coassociative, and the cobar
recovers an augmented associative algebra with empty component $\C$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The Ran convolution is symmetric and exact. Its positive $r$-fold
words vanish on a configuration of cardinality less than $r$.
This finite configuration bound makes the normalized bar--cobar
comparison finite at every configuration.

Flattening a bar--cobar
word with $r$ original inputs gives $r-1$ gaps, each with the two
states ``inside a block'' and ``between blocks''. On the associated
graded the gap differential is a tensor product of two-term identity
complexes. It is contractible for $r>1$. The empty and one-input
terms survive. The finite filtration proves the unit and counit
equivalences, including their coherent versions.

For the envelope, the comparison from the Chevalley--Eilenberg
coalgebra to the bar is suspended symmetrization
\[
 sx_1\cdots sx_r\longmapsto
 \sum_{\sigma\in S_r}\varepsilon(\sigma;sx_1,\ldots,sx_r)
 [x_{\sigma(1)}|\cdots|x_{\sigma(r)}].
\]
It has no factor $1/r!$: the unshuffle coproduct on the source
corresponds to deconcatenation of the permutation sum. Its binary
differential is the envelope commutator relation. The PBW filtration
reduces it to the abelian Koszul comparison, contracted in positive
polynomial weight by the Euler homotopy divided by that weight.
Characteristic zero and the finite configuration bound give an
equivalence. These constructions in the Ran coefficient category
are described in \cite{ChiralBarCobarVolI}.

Finally, the Chevalley--Eilenberg coalgebra of a chiral Lie object
satisfies factorization precisely when that Lie object is supported
on the main diagonal \cite[Theorems~5.1.1--5.2.1]{FrancisGaitsgoryCKD}.
Here it is $\iota_*V_C$, so the hypothesis holds. The associative
cobar assertion follows from the first paragraph.
\end{proof}

The Francis--Gaitsgory equivalence in the last step is between Lie
and commutative coalgebra objects. The associative statement uses
the envelope and its associative bar comparison in addition.
It does not construct a second independently defined chiral field
algebra.

Likewise, restriction to ordered words, pullback to a
collision atlas, and transposition of coefficient modules are
different operations. A transposed algebra needs a duality domain
and the correct exchanged extensions, such as $j_*$ and $j_!$.
The generally infinite current coefficients are not automatically
bounded holonomic objects. None of these operations turns a scalar
supercharacter into a map of coefficient systems.

\section{The character and the weight}
\label{sec:bkm-maass-multiplier}

The automorphic character is part of the denominator normalization.
For $g=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}A&B\\C&D\end{smallmatrix}\right)
\in\Sp_4(\Z)$ and $j(g,Z)=\det(CZ+D)$, one has
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bkm-delta5-transformation}
 \Delta_5(gZ)=\nu_{\Delta_5}(g)j(g,Z)^5\Delta_5(Z),
 \qquad \nu_{\Delta_5}^2=1.
\end{equation}
Consequently $\Delta_5^2$ has weight ten and trivial character.
The homogeneous orthogonal character
$\widetilde\nu_{\Delta_5}$ is the one in
Definition~\ref{def:homogeneous-orthogonal-character}. Its central
value at $-\Id$ is $-1$.

\begin{lemma}[Reflection values]
\label{lem:bkm-maass-reflection-values}
The character is $-1$ on each $s_{\delta_i}$ and $+1$ on each
of the three type-I chamber reflections.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Their symplectic lifts $g_\beta$ satisfy
$\sigma(g_\beta)=-s_\beta$ and $j(g_\beta,Z)=-1$.
Maass's character \cite{MAASS:1}, in the explicit form
\cite[(1.5), Propositions~2.1--2.2]{GN}, has values $+1$ on the
type-II lifts and $-1$ on the type-I lifts.
Multiplication by the homogeneous central value $-1$ gives the
claimed reflection values.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Linear action and slash action]
\label{lem:bkm-maass-restriction}
For $h=wa\in W\rtimes\Aut(\Poly_{II})$, one has
\[
 \widetilde\nu_{\Delta_5}|_W=\det,\qquad
 \widetilde\nu_{\Delta_5}|_{\Aut(\Poly_{II})}=1,
 \qquad \Delta_5(hZ)=\det(w)\Delta_5(Z).
\]
For a lift $g_h$ obtained by multiplying the six reflection lifts,
$j(g_h,Z)=\det(w)\det(a)$ and
\[
 (\Delta_5|_5g_h)(Z)=\nu_{\Delta_5}(g_h)\Delta_5(Z)
                     =\det(a)\Delta_5(Z).
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The generator values prove the restrictions and linear action.
The cocycle rule multiplies the constant factors $-1$ of the lifts,
giving $j(g_h,Z)=\det(h)$. Dividing the linear action by its fifth
power gives the slash action. Changing a lift by $-\Id_4$ leaves
these factors unchanged.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Eight Coxeter characters]
\label{rem:bkm-why-determinant}
Since the only Coxeter relations are $s_i^2=1$, a character
$W\to\{\pm1\}$ can choose each of its three signs independently.
There are eight such characters. The reflection calculation selects
$(-1,-1,-1)$, hence the determinant. Rescaling $\Delta_5$ cannot
alter these values. Identifying them with transport on a compact
orientation line still requires that line and its equivariant
comparison with the automorphic line.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Borcherds weight}
\label{sec:bkm-kappa-borcherds-weight}
\label{sec:bkm-volIII-frame}

The notation $\kappa_{\mathrm{BKM}}$ will mean the modular weight
of a specified Borcherds product. It is distinct from a categorical
trace, a current-algebra parameter or an Euler characteristic.

\begin{theorem}[Weight of the denominator]
\label{thm:bkm-kappa-universal}
For an admissible Borcherds input with constant coefficient
$c(0,0)$, the product has weight $c(0,0)/2$. In the present case,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bkm-kappa-formula}
 \kappa_{\mathrm{BKM}}(\Delta_5)=\frac{f(0,0)}2=\frac{10}2=5.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Apply \cite[Theorem~13.3]{B} to the weight-$-1/2$ Weil input
obtained by theta decomposition of $\phi_{0,1}$ on $\La^{3,2}$.
Its constant coefficient is ten. The exceptional orthogonal
identification transports this weight to the Siegel form, as in
Chapter~\ref{ch:singular-theta-lift}.
\end{proof}

For comparison, the cyclic CHL eta products at orders
$1,2,3,4,6$ have weights $12,8,6,5,4$:
\[
\begin{array}{c|l|c|c}
 N&g_N&\operatorname{wt}(g_N)&
       (\operatorname{wt}(g_N)-2)/2\\\hline
 1&\eta^{24}&12&5\\
 2&\eta^8\eta(2\tau)^8&8&3\\
 3&\eta^6\eta(3\tau)^6&6&2\\
 4&\eta^4\eta(2\tau)^2\eta(4\tau)^4&5&3/2\\
 6&\eta^2\eta(2\tau)^2\eta(3\tau)^2\eta(6\tau)^2&4&1
\end{array}
\]
Here unlabeled eta factors have argument $\tau$. Multiplication
by $\vartheta^2/\eta^6$ subtracts two from the seed weight. The last
column is its half-weight.

The lifting prescriptions and their
characters are specified in \cite{JS06Dyon,GK09CHL,GK10Composite}.
For the corresponding normalized square-root Borcherds inputs, the
weight formula reads $c_N(0)=(10,6,4,3,2)$ and gives precisely that
column. Half-integral weight requires the appropriate cover.

The frame shapes also describe the permutation action on twenty-four
coordinates \cite{ChengHarrison}. The number of fixed coordinates
is not this constant coefficient.

These cyclic orders do not index the eight pairs of the
Gritsenko--Cl\'ery catalog. In its input-pair order
\[
 (1,1),(2,1),(1,2),(3,1),(1,3),(4,1),(1,4),(2,2),
\]
the weights are $(5,2,3,1,2,\tfrac12,\tfrac32,1)$ and the
Borcherds constants are $(10,4,6,2,4,1,3,2)$
\cite[Theorem~1.4]{GC}. Appendix~\ref{app:borcherds-product-expansion}
gives the exact cusp and parameter dictionary. The common
$(1,1)$ member is the present $\Delta_5$.

\begin{remark}[Different characteristics]
\label{rem:bkm-additive-collapse-fails}
The numerical tuple $(0,0,3,5,24)$ can record proposed categorical
and chiral characteristics together with the Cartan rank, Borcherds
weight and K3 Euler number. The last three numbers have the present
interpretations
$\operatorname{rk}L=3$, $\operatorname{wt}\Delta_5=5$ and
$\chi_{\mathrm{top}}(K3)=24$. A categorical or chiral trace in
either first slot requires its own object and trace definition.
The Hodge Euler characteristic of $K3\times E$ is zero because
$\chi(\mathcal O_E)=0$, while $\chi(\mathcal O_{K3})=2$.
Reading a proposed additive formula through these Euler numbers gives
\[
 5\ne0+2.
\]

The different numerical equality $3+2=5$ uses the Cartan rank.
It cannot supply the varying CHL weights $5,3,2,3/2,1$ from a
fixed rank-three lattice and the same K3 Euler number.
Thus neither equality constructs an additive identity among these
independently defined characteristics.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Two inputs to the weight formula]
\label{rem:bkm-two-inputs-one-weight-rule}
\label{rem:bkm-paramodular-fake-monster-rows}
The present input has constant ten and weight five.
The fake-monster denominator on the Lorentzian lattice
$\mathrm{II}_{25,1}$ comes from $\eta^{-24}$, whose constant
coefficient is twenty-four, and has weight twelve
\cite{BorcherdsGKM88,Borcherds95}. Its orthogonal automorphic
product lives on the corresponding signature-$(26,2)$ extension.
The values five and twelve are evaluations of the same weight rule
on different lattices and different inputs.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Arithmetic consequences]

\begin{remark}[Boundary algebra and center]
\label{rem:bkm-target-not-bulk}
\label{rem:bkm-universal-holography-scope}
Suppose a factorization category $\mathcal C$ and a chosen boundary
object $b$ have been constructed. Its boundary algebra is
$A_b=R\operatorname{End}_{\mathcal C}(b)$ in the specified
coefficient recipient. A proposed bulk description by its derived
center requires the bulk-to-center map, its operation compatibility
and its reconstruction hypotheses. An identification with Hochschild
cochains uses the corresponding enrichment and representability
conditions \cite{ChiralBarCobarVolI,ChiralBarCobarVolII}.
Where a chiral Swiss-cheese brace action is defined, the comparison
must intertwine its mixed bulk-boundary operations. A gravitational
interpretation additionally requires line observables and their
operator product.

The root algebra $\mathfrak g$ is not, by its denominator identity,
that center. Nor may a bar coalgebra $C_X$ be substituted for the
associative boundary algebra in a center expression without a
proved comparison in the relevant category.

For a supplied compact coalgebra in $\mathcal R_E^+$, the current
construction provides a precise possible comparison target,
\[
 C_X\longrightarrow B\mathcal A_{\Delta,E},
 \qquad
 \Omega C_X\longrightarrow\mathcal A_{\Delta,E}.
\]
A constructible Hall system must first be realized as such a
right differential-module family. The morphism must preserve the
collision operations, the coaugmentation and the transition maps.
Its cone must be acyclic before it is called an equivalence.
A word-cut coalgebra gives only its specified ordered restriction.
The missing comparison with full collision diagrams remains part
of the compact problem.
\end{remark}

The local current vacuum $\omega_E\to V_E$, empty convolution
unit $K$, augmentation to $K$, and prepared physical state have
four different roles. A protected operator trace
requires a state or module, its action and its trace functional.
Neither the current construction nor the scalar product chooses
that state. The compact recognition problem requires these coefficient and
operation maps together with the invariant Lie comparison. Their
completion must support all the stated operations. The algebraic constructions of this chapter fix their
codomains and their first nontrivial root tests.
