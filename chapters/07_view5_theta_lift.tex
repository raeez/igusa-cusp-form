\chapter{The singular theta lift}
\label{ch:singular-theta-lift}

\section{From Jacobi coefficients to an automorphic section}
\label{sec:singular-theta-lift}
\label{ssec:theta-lift-notation}

The product in Chapter~\ref{ch:view1-automorphic} gives a holomorphic
section from the coefficients of \(\phi_{0,1}\).
The singular theta lift explains why the product is automorphic.
Its integral first produces a real function on an orthogonal
symmetric domain.  This function determines the norm of a
meromorphic section.  A monic cusp normalization fixes its phase
and identifies the section with \(D_5\).

Three normalizations will enter.  The positive leading coefficient
fixes \(D_5\).  The specified theta constants
give \(\Delta_5=64D_5\).  The root-denominator character convention
gives \(\operatorname{den}(\autcor)(Z)=D_5(2Z)\).
We derive the lattice maps and metric factors that relate these
expressions.

\subsection{The Jacobi form}
\label{ssec:phi01}

\begin{definition}[Weak Jacobi form]
\label{def:weak-jacobi-phi01}
Fix integers \(k\) and \(t>0\).
A weak Jacobi form of weight \(k\) and index \(t\) is a
holomorphic function \(\phi:\mathbb H_1\times\C\to\C\)
satisfying
\[
 \phi\!\left(\frac{a\tau+b}{c\tau+d},\frac z{c\tau+d}\right)
 =(c\tau+d)^k e^{2\pi itcz^2/(c\tau+d)}\phi(\tau,z)
\]
for \(\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)
\in\SL_2(\Z)\), and
\[
 \phi(\tau,z+\lambda\tau+\mu)
 =e^{-2\pi it(\lambda^2\tau+2\lambda z)}\phi(\tau,z)
 \quad(\lambda,\mu\in\Z).
\]
At the cusp its Fourier expansion has the form
\(\sum_{n\geq0,\ell\in\Z}f(n,\ell)q^nr^\ell\).
\end{definition}

The elliptic law identifies coefficients with the same
\((4tn-\ell^2,\ell\bmod2t)\).  At index one the residue is
determined by the discriminant.  The normalization
\(r^{-1}+10+r+O(q)\) singles out \(\phi_{0,1}\).

\begin{lemma}[Theta-quotient presentation]
\label{lem:phi01-presentations}
With the genus-one theta functions fixed in
Chapter~\ref{ch:view1-automorphic},
\[
 \phi_{0,1}(\tau,z)
 =4\left(
 \frac{\theta_2(\tau,z)^2}{\theta_2(\tau,0)^2}
 +\frac{\theta_3(\tau,z)^2}{\theta_3(\tau,0)^2}
 +\frac{\theta_4(\tau,z)^2}{\theta_4(\tau,0)^2}\right).
\]
Its coefficients are those in
\eqref{eq:view1-phi01-coefficients}, and
\(f(n,\ell)=0\) for \(4n-\ell^2<-1\).
Moreover \(\phi_{0,1}(\tau,0)=12\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The modular transformations permute the three even theta quotients
and give their common index-one exponential factor.
The elliptic transformations give the second law in the definition.
Their \(q^0\) sum is \(r^{-1}+10+r\).
The one-dimensionality of \(J_{0,1}^{\mathrm{weak}}\) therefore
identifies the sum \cite{EZ:1}.
Coefficient recurrence gives the support bound, and setting \(z=0\)
gives \(4(1+1+1)=12\).
The expansion in Appendix~\ref{app:borcherds-product-expansion}
computes the displayed Fourier table directly.
\end{proof}

The classes of discriminants \(0,4,-1\), respectively, give
\[
 f(0,0)=f(1,\pm2)=10,\quad
 f(1,0)=f(2,\pm2)=108,\quad
 f(0,\pm1)=f(2,\pm3)=1.
\]
They are coefficient identities of the Jacobi form.
The factor \(64\) in the genus-two theta product is a separate
normalization calculation.

\section{The orthogonal domain}
\label{ssec:lattice-3-2}

The lift uses a rank-five lattice.  Its explicit construction also
determines the relation between the symplectic and orthogonal
automorphic lines.

\begin{definition}[The exterior-square lattice]
\label{def:lambda-3-2}
Let \(\Lambda^4=\bigoplus_{i=1}^4\Z e_i\), with volume
\(\mathrm{vol}=e_1\wedge e_2\wedge e_3\wedge e_4\).
On \(\wedge^2\Lambda^4\) define
\(u\wedge v=(u,v)\mathrm{vol}\).
Put
\[
 q_0=e_1\wedge e_3+e_2\wedge e_4,\qquad
 L=\Lambda^{3,2}=q_0^\perp.
\]
\end{definition}

The pairing on the exterior square is even and unimodular of
signature \((3,3)\), and \(q_0^2=-2\).
An integral basis of its orthogonal complement is
\[
\begin{aligned}
 f_1&=e_1\wedge e_2,&
 f_2&=e_2\wedge e_3,&
 f_3&=e_1\wedge e_3-e_2\wedge e_4,\\
 f_{-2}&=e_4\wedge e_1,&
 f_{-1}&=e_4\wedge e_3.
\end{aligned}
\]
Indeed the orthogonality equation sets the coefficients of
\(e_1\wedge e_3\) and \(e_2\wedge e_4\) to opposite values.
The remaining four coefficients are arbitrary integers.

In this basis the Gram matrix is
\[
 G_L=
 \begin{pmatrix}
 0&0&0&0&-1\\
 0&0&0&-1&0\\
 0&0&2&0&0\\
 0&-1&0&0&0\\
 -1&0&0&0&0
 \end{pmatrix}.
\]
Thus
\[
 L=\HypPlan\oplus\HypPlan\oplus[2],\qquad
 \HypPlan=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix},
 \qquad \det G_L=2.
\]
Its discriminant group is \(\Z/2\Z\), generated by
\(f_3/2+L\), with quadratic value \(1/4\bmod\Z\).

The type-IV domain consists of the negative null lines
\[
 \mathbb H^{\mathrm{IV}}
 =\{[w]\in\mathbb P(L\otimes\C):(w,w)=0,\quad
                                  (w,\overline w)<0\}.
\]
Choose the component containing the vectors
\[
 w(Z)=(z_2^2-z_1z_3,\ z_3,\ z_2,\ z_1,\ 1),
 \qquad Z\in\mathbb H_2,
\]
written in the \(f\)-basis.  Direct pairing gives
\[
 (w(Z),w(Z))_L=0,\qquad
 (w(Z),\overline{w(Z)})_L=-4\det(\Imag Z).
\]
The negative real plane spanned by the real and imaginary parts of
\(w(Z)\), with this order, determines its orientation.

\subsection{The exceptional isomorphism}
\label{ssec:exceptional-iso}

The stabilizer of \(q_0\) in \(\SL_4\) is the symplectic group for
\[
 J_4(x,y)\,\mathrm{vol}=-x\wedge y\wedge q_0.
\]
It preserves \(L\).
Let \(\Gamma_{3,2}\) be the projective exterior-square image
of \(\Sp_4(\Z)\)
in \(\mathrm{PO}^+(L)\), the group preserving the chosen domain
component.  Since \(L\) has odd rank, each projective isometry has
a unique representative of determinant one.  We use that
representative when acting on the cone.
The integral determinant-one isometries preserving this component
form \(\mathrm{SO}^+(L)\).

\begin{theorem}[Exterior-square exceptional isomorphism]
\label{thm:exceptional-iso}
The exterior-square action induces
\[
 \Sp_4(\Z)/\{\pm I_4\}
 \simeq\Gamma_{3,2}
 \simeq\mathrm{SO}^+(L).
\]
The map \(Z\mapsto[w(Z)]\) identifies \(\mathbb H_2\)
with the chosen component \(\mathbb H^{\mathrm{IV}}_+\).
For \(g\in\Sp_4(\R)\), its homogeneous lifts satisfy
\[
 (\wedge^2g)w(Z)=j(g,Z)\,w(gZ),
 \qquad j(g,Z)=\det(CZ+D).
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The exterior-square pairing is \(\SL_4\)-invariant.
The stabilizer description therefore gives the homomorphism.
Its kernel on \(q_0^\perp\) is \(\{\pm I_4\}\).
The integral image is \(\mathrm{SO}^+(L)\)
by Gritsenko--Nikulin's integral identification
\cite[Lemma 1.1]{GN}.

For the action on the domain, let \(Q_1(Z),Q_2(Z)\) be the columns
of \(\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}Z\\I_2\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\).
Their exterior product satisfies
\(w(Z)=-Q_1(Z)\wedge Q_2(Z)\).
The matrix identity
\[
 g\begin{pmatrix}Z\\I_2\end{pmatrix}
 =\begin{pmatrix}gZ\\I_2\end{pmatrix}(CZ+D)
\]
proves the homogeneous transformation formula.

The null equation and negativity give
\(\det(\Imag Z)>0\).  The selected component imposes
\(\Imag z_1>0\), hence \(\Imag Z>0\).
Conversely each negative null line in this component has the
displayed affine representative.  Its last coordinate cannot vanish:
the orthogonal complement of an isotropic real vector has negative
index one and contains no negative definite two-plane.
This proves the domain identification.
\end{proof}

The corresponding real-group isomorphisms are
\cite[Chapter VI, Problems 25--27]{KNAPP:1}
\[
 \PSp_4(\R)\simeq\mathrm{SO}_0(3,2),\qquad
 \Sp_4(\R)\simeq\Spin(3,2).
\]
Both groups give the same symmetric domain, with maximal compact
groups \(\Un(2)\) and \(\mathrm{SO}(3)\times\mathrm{SO}(2)\)
related by the spin covering.  This covering concerns the output
group.  The metaplectic group \(\Mp_2(\Z)\) below acts on the
one-dimensional input upper half-plane.

A homogeneous function \(\Psi\) of degree \(-k\) on the null cone
defines \(F(Z)=\Psi(w(Z))\).  If
\(\Psi((\wedge^2g)w)=\chi(g)\Psi(w)\), the theorem gives
\[
 F(gZ)=\chi(g)\,j(g,Z)^k F(Z).
\]
This proves the identification of the two automorphic lines,
including their characters.

\section{The Weil input}
\label{ssec:phi-to-weil}

Borcherds's integral uses a lattice of signature \((2,3)\).
Set \(M=L(-1)\): its underlying abelian group is \(L\), and
its pairing is the negative of the one above.  Write
\[
 Q_M(v)=\frac{(v,v)_M}{2},\qquad
 A_M=M^*/M=\{0,\gamma\},\qquad
 Q_M(\gamma)=-\frac14\bmod\Z.
\]
The standard basis of \(\C[A_M]\) is \(e_0,e_\gamma\).

For \(e(t)=e^{2\pi it}\), the Weil representation \(\rho_M\)
of \(\Mp_2(\Z)\) is given on the standard lifts by
\[
 \rho_M(T)=
 \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-i\end{pmatrix},\qquad
 \rho_M(S)=\frac{e(1/8)}{\sqrt2}
             \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Here
\[
 T=\left(\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix},1\right),\qquad
 S=\left(\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\sqrt{\tau}\right),
 \quad 0<\arg\sqrt{\tau}<\frac{\pi}{2}.
\]
The hyperbolic summands are unimodular, so these are the
discriminant matrices of the rank-one lattice \([-2]\).
In particular \(\rho_M=\rho_L^\vee\).

\begin{lemma}[Theta decomposition]
\label{lem:theta-decomposition}
For
\[
 \theta_{1,\mu}(\tau,z)
 =\sum_{\ell\equiv\mu\ (2)}q^{\ell^2/4}r^\ell,\qquad \mu=0,1,
\]
there are unique functions \(h_0,h_1\) such that
\[
 \phi_{0,1}=h_0\theta_{1,0}+h_1\theta_{1,1},\qquad
 [q^{n-\ell^2/4}]h_{\ell\bmod2}=f(n,\ell).
\]
The vector \(h=h_0e_0+h_1e_\gamma\), also denoted
\(\phi_{0,1}^{\mathrm{Weil}}\), is a weakly holomorphic
modular form of weight \(-\tfrac12\) and representation \(\rho_M\).
Its first terms are
\[
 h_0=10+108q+808q^2+O(q^3),\qquad
 h_1=q^{-1/4}(1-64q-513q^2+O(q^3)).
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The elliptic coefficient recurrence identifies all exponents with the
same discriminant and residue.  Grouping the even and odd powers
gives the decomposition and uniqueness.
Poisson summation gives the theta vector weight \(\tfrac12\)
and Weil representation \(\rho_L\).
Since its contraction with \(h\) has weight zero and trivial
representation, \(h\) has the dual representation and weight
\(-\tfrac12\) \cite[Theorem 5.1]{EZ:1}.
For example,
\(\theta_{1,1}(\tau+1,z)=i\theta_{1,1}(\tau,z)\), forcing
\(h_1(\tau+1)=-ih_1(\tau)\).
The coefficient table proves the displayed expansions.
\end{proof}

The support bound has become a simple principal part:
the only negative-index coefficient of \(h\) is
\(q^{-1/4}e_\gamma\), with coefficient \(1\).
The constant coefficient of the zero component is \(10\).
These two numbers will determine the divisor and the weight.

\section{The regularized integral}
\label{ssec:borcherds-theta-correspondence}
\label{ssec:singular-theta}

An oriented negative two-plane of \(L\) is an oriented positive
two-plane of \(M\).  For such a plane \(P\), decompose
\(\lambda=\lambda_++\lambda_-\) by orthogonal projection to
\(P\) and \(P^\perp\).  The second summand has negative definite
pairing.

\begin{definition}[Siegel theta series]
\label{def:siegel-theta}
For \(\tau=u+iv\in\mathbb H_1\), define
\[
 \Theta_M(\tau,P)=
 \sum_{\delta\in A_M}e_\delta
 \sum_{\lambda\in M+\delta}
 \exp\!\left(
 \pi i\tau(\lambda_+,\lambda_+)_M
 +\pi i\overline\tau(\lambda_-,\lambda_-)_M\right).
\]
\end{definition}

Each summand has absolute value
\[
 \exp\!\left[-\pi v
 \bigl((\lambda_+,\lambda_+)_M-(\lambda_-,\lambda_-)_M\bigr)\right].
\]
The expression in parentheses is positive definite.
This proves convergence of the kernel in every lattice direction.
Its modular weight is \((1,\tfrac32)\) and its representation is
\(\rho_M\).

Let \(F=\sum_\delta F_\delta e_\delta\) be weakly holomorphic
of weight \(-\tfrac12\) for \(\rho_M\).
Write
\[
 F_\delta(\tau)=\sum_{a\in\Q}c_\delta(a)q^a,
 \qquad c_\delta(a)=0\ \text{for }a\ll0,
\]
and suppose \(c_\delta(a)\in\Z\) for \(a\leq0\).
Use the Hermitian contraction
\[
 \langle F,\Theta_M\rangle
 =\sum_\delta F_\delta\,\overline{\Theta_{M,\delta}}.
\]
Its weight is \((1,1)\), since
\((-\tfrac12,0)+(\tfrac32,1)=(1,1)\).
Multiplication by \(v\) makes it invariant.

For the usual modular fundamental domain truncated at \(v=T\),
denoted \(\mathcal F_T\), set
\[
 \Phi_M(P,F)=\operatorname{CT}_{s=0}
 \left[
 \lim_{T\to\infty}
 \int_{\mathcal F_T}
  v\langle F(\tau),\Theta_M(\tau,P)\rangle v^{-s}
  \frac{du\,dv}{v^2}
 \right].
\]
The limit is taken first in the convergent half-plane and then
continued meromorphically in \(s\).
The constant Laurent coefficient is Borcherds's regularization
\cite[\S6]{B}.
The resulting function is evaluated away from its rational quadratic
singularities.  In particular, the conjugated kernel and the power
of \(v\) are part of the definition.

To state the norm identity, write \(w=X_M+iY_M\) on the positive
null cone of \(M\), and put
\[
 P(w)=\operatorname{span}_{\R}(X_M,Y_M),\qquad
 |Y_M|=\sqrt{(Y_M,Y_M)_M},\qquad
 C_B=\frac{\Gamma'(1)}2+\log\sqrt{2\pi}.
\]
The null equation says that \(X_M\) and \(Y_M\) are orthogonal
with equal positive norm.

\begin{theorem}[Singular theta lift {\cite[Theorem 13.3]{B}}]
\label{thm:singular-theta-lift}
For the input \(F\) above, put \(k=c_0(0)/2\).
There is a meromorphic homogeneous function \(\Psi_M(w,F)\)
of degree \(-k\), with the following properties.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\arabic*)}]
\item It is an automorphic form of weight \(k\), with a unitary
character, for the component-preserving isometries of \(M\)
that preserve \(F\).
\item Its order along the divisor orthogonal to a rational
negative line \(\R\lambda\) is
\[
 \operatorname{ord}_{\lambda^\perp}\Psi_M
 =\sum_{\substack{x>0\\x\lambda\in M^*}}
 c_{x\lambda+M}\!\left(x^2Q_M(\lambda)\right).
\]
Each divisor occurs once in this formula, and all contributing
positive multiples are included.
Nonnegative orders imply holomorphy in this rank-five case.
\item Its norm satisfies
\[
 \log|\Psi_M(w,F)|
 =-\frac14\Phi_M(P(w),F)
       -k\bigl(\log|Y_M|+C_B\bigr).
\]
Equivalently, for
\(\|\Psi_M\|=|\Psi_M(w,F)|\,|Y_M|^k\),
\[
 \Phi_M(P,F)=-4\log\|\Psi_M\|-4kC_B.
\]
\item Choose a primitive isotropic \(z\) in a unimodular
hyperbolic summand and an isotropic \(z'\in M\) with
\((z,z')_M=1\).  Let
\(K=(M\cap z^\perp)/\Z z\), identified with the orthogonal
complement of their span.  Thus \(K\) has signature \((1,2)\)
and \(A_K=A_M\).
In the cusp section \(w=z'+\zeta-Q_M(\zeta)z\), the product is
\[
 \Psi_M(w,F)
 =C\,e\bigl((\rho_{\mathrm{Bor}},\zeta)_M\bigr)
 \prod_{\substack{\lambda\in K^*\\(\lambda,W)_M>0}}
 \left(1-e\bigl((\lambda,\zeta)_M\bigr)\right)^{
                  c_{\lambda+K}(Q_M(\lambda))}.
\]
Here \(W\) is a Weyl chamber, \(\rho_{\mathrm{Bor}}\) its
Weyl vector, and \(|C|=1\).
The product converges sufficiently near the cusp with
\(\Imag\zeta\in W\).
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

The assertion about \(|C|\) uses the primitive unimodular hyperbolic
summand in the statement.  For a general cusp, the product constant
also contains the finite factors in
\cite[Theorem 13.3(5)]{B}.
The integral fixes an absolute norm.  At the present cusp we choose
the remaining phase so that \(C=1\), and denote the resulting
product by \(\mathcal B_M(F)\).

\begin{theorem}[Dependence on the input]
\label{thm:regularized-theta-correspondence}
In a common cusp chamber, the monic products satisfy
\[
 \mathcal B_M(F_1+F_2)=\mathcal B_M(F_1)\mathcal B_M(F_2).
\]
Their weights and divisor orders add, and their characters multiply.
The zero input gives the constant function \(1\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The integral is linear in \(F\).
In the cusp product each exponent is a Fourier coefficient of \(F\),
and the Weyl vector depends linearly on this input.
The normalized products therefore multiply factor by factor.
Continuation proves the equality of meromorphic sections.
The weight and divisor formulas give the remaining assertions.
\end{proof}

The target thus varies with \(F\).
A general multiplicative lift takes values in meromorphic sections
of different weights and characters.  The form of weight five
arises from the particular vector \(h\).

\section{The Igusa specialization}
\label{ssec:theta-lift-delta5}

Choose \(z=f_1\), \(z'=f_{-1}\) in \(M\).
Then
\[
 K=\Z f_2\oplus\Z f_3\oplus\Z f_{-2},\qquad
 \zeta=z_3f_2+z_2f_3+z_1f_{-2}.
\]
For \((n,\ell,m)\in\Z^3\), define
\[
 \lambda(n,\ell,m)
 =nf_2-\frac{\ell}{2}f_3+mf_{-2}\in K^*.
\]
The coordinate and quadratic identities are
\[
 (\lambda,\zeta)_M=nz_1+\ell z_2+mz_3,\qquad
 Q_M(\lambda)=nm-\frac{\ell^2}{4}.
\]
The class of \(\lambda\) in \(A_K\) is \(\ell\bmod2\).
The theta decomposition therefore identifies the exponent:
\[
 c_{\lambda+K}(Q_M(\lambda))=f(nm,\ell).
\]

In this formula the zero coefficients allow \(n,m\) to range over
all integers.  A nonzero coefficient has
\(4nm-\ell^2\geq-1\).
In the chamber \(y_1+y_2>0\), \(y_3+y_2>0\), \(y_2<0\),
the positive covectors on this support are precisely the
\(\lambda(n,\ell,m)\) with
\((n,\ell,m)\in\Gamma_{\mathrm{act}}\).
Indeed \(nm<0\) is excluded by the support bound.
For \(n,m>0\), the positive-cone calculation of
Chapter~\ref{ch:view1-automorphic} gives the claimed sign.
The case \(n,m<0\) has the opposite sign.

If \(nm=0\), the allowed elliptic exponents are \(0,\pm1\),
and the same inequalities decide the sign directly.

\subsection{The two Weyl-vector coordinates}
\label{ssec:weyl-vector-rho}

\begin{theorem}[Weyl vector of the lift]
\label{thm:weyl-vector-lift}
The cusp exponents of \(\mathcal B_M(h)\) are
\((A,B,C)=(\tfrac12,\tfrac12,\tfrac12)\).
In the \(e((\lambda,\zeta)_M)\) convention of the integral,
\[
 \rho_{\mathrm{Bor}}
 =\frac12f_2-\frac14f_3+\frac12f_{-2}.
\]
In the root convention
\(e^{-\pi i(\alpha,\zeta)_L}\), the vector is
\[
 \rho=2\rho_{\mathrm{Bor}}
 =f_2-\frac12f_3+f_{-2}
 =\frac12(\delta_1+\delta_2+\delta_3).
\]
It satisfies \((\rho,\delta_i)_L=-1\) for \(i=1,2,3\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The Jacobi product theorem \cite[Theorem 2.1]{GNII} gives
\[
 A=\frac1{24}\sum_\ell f(0,\ell),\qquad
 B=\frac12\sum_{\ell>0}\ell f(0,\ell),\qquad
 C=\frac14\sum_\ell\ell^2 f(0,\ell).
\]
The row \(r^{-1}+10+r\) makes all three numbers \(1/2\).
The vector \(\lambda(A,B,C)\) is \(\rho_{\mathrm{Bor}}\).
Since \(\alpha(n,\ell,m)=2\lambda(n,\ell,m)\) and
\((\, ,\,)_M=-(\, ,\,)_L\), the root vector is twice this
covector.

The simple-root Gram matrix has diagonal entries \(2\) and
off-diagonal entries \(-2\).  Its row sums are \(-2\), proving
\((\rho,\delta_i)_L=-1\).
Its eigenvalues are \(-2,4,4\), so its determinant is \(-32\).
The simple roots form an integral basis of \(M_{II}\), and these
integral pairings show \(\rho\in M_{II}^*\).
\end{proof}

The same half-integral exponent vector is therefore represented by
different vectors in the two exponential conventions.
The factor of two belongs to the pairing formula, before the
separate substitution \(Z\mapsto2Z\) in the denominator.

\begin{theorem}[The Igusa form as multiplicative lift]
\label{thm:delta5-as-theta-lift}
The phase-normalized multiplicative lift of \(h\) is \(D_5\):
\[
 \mathcal B_M(h)(w(Z))=D_5(Z).
\]
In the fixed theta convention define
\(\Theta(h)=\Theta_{\mathrm{sing}}^{\mathrm{Bor}}(h)
:=64\,\mathcal B_M(h)\).
Then
\[
 \Theta(h)(w(Z))=\Delta_5(Z).
\]
The notation \(\Theta(h)\) denotes this multiplicative section,
whereas \(\Phi_M(P,h)\) denotes the regularized additive function.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The cusp covector formula identifies each product exponent.
The Weyl-vector theorem identifies the prefactor \((qrs)^{1/2}\).
Both products have leading coefficient \(1\), so
Theorem~\ref{thm:view1-borcherds-gritsenko-nikulin-lift}
identifies the monic lift with \(D_5\) on the convergence region.
Continuation gives the global equality.
The theta coefficient then gives the second equality.
\end{proof}

The divisor formula can also be read directly from the input.
Its only negative coefficient is \(c_\gamma(-1/4)=1\).
Thus a divisor is supported on a vector
\(\lambda\in M^*\) with \(Q_M(\lambda)=-1/4\).
Every smaller positive multiple has quadratic value strictly between
\(-1/4\) and \(0\), impossible in \(Q_M(M^*)\subset\frac14\Z\).
Larger multiples have zero negative coefficient.
Each supported divisor has order one.

Equivalently, \(\delta=2\lambda\in L\) has norm \(2\) and
divisibility two.  A type-I norm-two vector has divisibility one,
so its half does not lie in the dual lattice and its divisor has
order zero.  The supported divisors are the translates of the
diagonal identified by the Igusa product.  The absence of poles,
the weight \(5\), and the cusp form identity agree with the
general lift theorem.

\subsection{The Petersson norm in Siegel coordinates}

The cusp section in the theorem is exactly \(w(Z)\), since
\[
 Q_M(\zeta)=z_1z_3-z_2^2,\qquad (w(Z),f_1)_M=1.
\]
Its imaginary part has last coordinate zero, and hence
\[
 (\Imag w(Z),\Imag w(Z))_M
 =2y_1y_3-2y_2^2=2\det(\Imag Z).
\]
The norm formula becomes
\[
 \Phi_M(P(Z),h)
 =-4\log\!\left(|D_5(Z)|[2\det(\Imag Z)]^{5/2}\right)
   -20C_B.
\]
For the theta-normalized form it is
\[
 \Phi_M(P(Z),h)
 =-4\log\!\left(|\Delta_5(Z)|[2\det(\Imag Z)]^{5/2}\right)
   +4\log64-20C_B.
\]
The additive constant therefore records both the chosen
regularization and the theta normalization.  A formula for the norm
does not determine the phase of the section.

\subsection{The character}
\label{ssec:consistency-conventions}

\begin{theorem}[Multiplier system of the lift]
\label{thm:multiplier-system}
On \(\Sp_4(\Z)\), the lift has Maass character \(\nu_5\).
Its Jacobi-parabolic restriction is
\(v_\eta^{12}\times v_H\).
For the linear tube action, the character is
\(\varepsilon_5|_{W^{(2)}(M_{II})}=\det\) and
\(\varepsilon_5|_{S_3}=1\).
It is the restriction of the homogeneous orthogonal character
\(\widetilde\nu_5\) of Definition~\ref{def:homogeneous-orthogonal-character}.
These characters are related on the chosen symplectic reflection
lifts by
\[
 \varepsilon_5(s_\beta)=\widetilde\nu_5(s_\beta)=-\nu_5(g_\beta).
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Equality with \(D_5\) gives the Maass character.
The first Fourier--Jacobi coefficient is
\(b=\eta^9\vartheta\).
The odd theta function has multiplier \(v_\eta^3\times v_H\),
giving the asserted parabolic restriction
\cite[(13)]{GC}.
The six reflection matrices and their weight factors are computed in
Lemma~\ref{lem:view1-maass-multiplier-restriction}.
\end{proof}

The finite Weil representation and the automorphic character enter
at different stages.  The former acts on the two input components.
The latter describes the output line.
Borcherds's theorem supplies a unitary character for each admissible
input.  Here its identification with the Maass character follows
from the product equality, including its phase.

The rank-three lattice \(M_0=\HypPlan\oplus[2]\) is a primitive
summand of \(L\), with orthogonal complement the second
hyperbolic plane.  The BKM grading lattice \(M_{II}\) has index
four in \(M_0\).
Thus \(L\) has rank five and discriminant two, while \(M_{II}\)
has rank three and determinant \(-32\).
The decomposition \(L\simeq\mathrm{II}_{2,2}\oplus[2]\)
is another way to write the two hyperbolic planes and the rank-one
summand.  It does not identify \(L\) with a unimodular lattice.

\section{The denominator and the weight}
\label{ssec:compat-view-ii}

\begin{theorem}[Lift--denominator compatibility]
\label{thm:lift-denominator}
For the Gritsenko--Nikulin algebra \(\autcor\) on \(M_{II}\),
\[
 \operatorname{den}(\autcor)(Z)
 =D_5(2Z)
 =e^{-2\pi i(\rho,\zeta)_L}
   \prod_{\alpha\in\mathcal R_+}
         (1-e^{-2\pi i(\alpha,\zeta)_L})^{\operatorname{smult}(\alpha)}.
\]
Here \(\mathcal R_+\) is the positive root set, and
\[
 \operatorname{smult}(\alpha(n,\ell,m))=f(nm,\ell)
 \quad\text{on }\Gamma_{\mathrm{act}}.
\]
Its negative-norm simple-root coefficients \(m(a)\) and isotropic
simple-root coefficients \(\tau(a)\) are those in the
Gritsenko--Nikulin presentation.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The algebra and its denominator theorem are constructed in
\cite[\S\S2--4 and \S6]{GN}.
The equality of exponents follows from the cusp covector calculation.
The root convention has
\(q^nr^\ell s^m=e^{-\pi i(\alpha,\zeta)_L}\).
Doubling \(Z\) changes this to the denominator character and doubles
the Weyl exponent in the same way.
This gives \(D_5(2Z)\).
\end{proof}

\subsection{Agreement of the three constructions}
\label{ssec:compat-view-i}

\begin{theorem}[Automorphic, root and theta identities]
\label{thm:bgn-three-way}
After the exceptional domain identification,
\[
 \mathcal D_X(Z)=\Theta(h)(w(Z))
 =64\,\operatorname{den}(\autcor)(Z/2)=\Delta_5(Z).
\]
All three expressions define the same section of
\(\mathcal L^5\otimes\nu_5\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The first equality uses the explicitly normalized lift.
The second is the denominator rescaling just proved.
The automorphic line identification follows from
\((\wedge^2g)w(Z)=j(g,Z)w(gZ)\).
\end{proof}

The equality \(\mathcal D_X=\Delta_5\) is the notation for the
automorphic target.  The substantive comparisons are the
multiplicative-lift theorem and the denominator theorem.
Neither changes the input category of the other construction.

\subsection{The weight coordinate}
\label{ssec:universal-weight-identity}

\begin{theorem}[The weight identity at index one]
\label{thm:universal-weight-identity}
For \(h=\phi_{0,1}^{\mathrm{Weil}}\),
\[
 \kappa_{\mathrm{BKM}}(\Delta_5)
 =\operatorname{wt}\mathcal B_M(h)
 =\frac{c_0(0)}2=\frac{f(0,0)}2=5.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The zero component of the theta decomposition has constant term
\(10\).  Apply the weight formula of
Theorem~\ref{thm:singular-theta-lift}.
\end{proof}

The eight-form catalog uses the same weight principle.
At congruence level, its vector-valued zero coefficient is assembled
from the Jacobi expansions at every cusp, as specified in
Appendix~\ref{app:borcherds-product-expansion}.
The pair \((t,N)\) must therefore accompany such a constant.
It cannot be replaced by a CHL order without identifying the actual
input.

The number \(5\) is independent of
\(e(S)=24\), \(\chi(\mathcal O_S)=2\), and the rank \(3\)
of the root-degree lattice.
An equality \(3+2=5\) alone constructs no comparison of the
associated operations.
Replacing the lattice rank by a chiral or Heisenberg invariant
requires the normalized bar, trace and comparison data of
Chapter~\ref{ch:pentadic}.

\section{Geometric comparison data}
\label{ssec:beilinson-cut-view-v}
\label{ssec:theta-lift-boundary}

The primitive input of this construction is \(h\), together with the
lattice, Weil representation and cusp.
The regularized integral determines a norm.
The multiplicative lift supplies a section and its automorphy,
and the cusp choice fixes the phase.
These passages have distinct mathematical content.

\begin{corollary}[The automorphic part of the realization problem]
\label{cor:five-views-coincidence}
The automorphic section, the rescaled BKM denominator and the
normalized multiplicative theta lift coincide as in
Theorem~\ref{thm:bgn-three-way}.
Their comparison with a compact Pfaffian additionally requires a
geometric parameter map, a descended line isomorphism and a
compatible section.  The mirror-discriminant comparison requires
its period map and divisor identification.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The identity is the preceding theorem.
The additional comparisons have their source lines and bases in
the compact and mirror constructions, respectively.
They require the displayed maps before equality of sections can
be formulated.
\end{proof}

In the sequence of Chapter~\ref{ch:pentadic}, the theta integral
is an operation on primitive inputs.
The section \(\Delta_5\) is its normalized multiplicative value
on \(h\).
The BKM algebra retains its bracket and root spaces, which its
denominator supercharacter forgets.
The relative Pfaffian assertion of
Appendix~\ref{app:obstruction-discharges} retains its D0-HN,
quotient-orientation, Weyl-transport, wall and finite Pfaffian
hypotheses.  A scalar trace comparison at the level of
Definition~\ref{def:G-vol2-hall-borcherds} does not supply an
operation-preserving Hall--Borcherds map.
Such a map, with its state and reduction data, is still needed for
the proposed compact operator or gravitational realization.

The output weight is integral in the present case.
Half-integral weights in other members of the catalog require a
square-root automorphy factor with its multiplier.
Equivalently, one uses a suitable cover of the output group.
This output choice is separate from the \(\Mp_2(\Z)\) action on
the Weil input.  The distinction preserves the character
information needed for any subsequent geometric line comparison.
